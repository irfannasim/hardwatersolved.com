The **best replacement water softener resin** is the grade your water calls for, bought in the volume your tank already holds. For most homes on chlorinated city water that means a 10 percent crosslink resin, such as ResinTech CG10 or AFWFilters' 1.5 cubic foot 10 percent refill kit. Clean, chlorine-free wells do just as well on standard 8 percent resin. Wells with dissolved iron suit fine mesh. On chloramine, no resin grade is enough without carbon in front of it.

The usual mistakes are buying by grain rating instead of cubic feet, and buying the cheapest grade for water that will wear it out in a few years. This page fixes both.

## Pick the Grade From Your Water, Not the Box

There are three grades worth knowing, and they differ in one way each. Standard resin is 8 percent crosslinked. Chlorine-resistant resin is the same polymer built tighter, at 10 percent. Fine mesh is standard resin in smaller beads. Total capacity per cubic foot is about the same for all three. What changes is how long the bed survives your water.

Ecolab Purolite's note on [factors affecting softening resin performance and life](https://www.purolite.com/index/core-technologies/application/softening/factors-effect-softening-resin-performance-life) gives the rule the industry plans with: each 1 mg/L of chlorine in the supply cuts resin life by about half. It also says higher crosslinking gives "greater resistance to oxidative attack". That is the whole case for the 10 percent grade. The arithmetic of when the premium pays is worked through in [standard vs chlorine-resistant softener resin](/blog/standard-vs-chlorine-resistant-softener-resin/), and the iron case for small beads is in [standard vs fine mesh resin](/blog/standard-vs-fine-mesh-resin/).

![Replacement water softener resin selector: city water by chlorine level to 8% or 10% crosslink resin or carbon plus 10%, and well water to standard, fine mesh or an iron filter first](/diagrams/replacement-softener-resin-selector-by-water-source.svg "The water test picks the grade. The tank label picks the quantity.")

Run two tests before you order. On city water, test **free and total chlorine** at a tap ahead of the softener, and use the highest reading you see, not the utility's average. If total runs well above free, the residual is mostly chloramine. On a well, get a lab test for **iron, manganese and pH**, and look for orange slime in the toilet tank, which points to iron bacteria.

## How Much Resin Your Tank Needs

Resin is sold by the cubic foot, and the tank holds a set volume of it. The grain rating on the old softener's box is a marketing figure worked back from that volume at a heavy salt dose, so do not buy from it. Read the volume from the tank label or manual. If neither survives, measure the tank's diameter and height and use the common pairings below.

| Tank (diameter x height, inches) | Resin it usually holds | Usually sold as | What to buy |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 x 44 | 0.75 cu ft | 24,000 grain | One 1 cu ft bag, part used |
| 9 x 48 | 1.0 cu ft | 32,000 grain | One 1 cu ft bag |
| 10 x 44 | 1.0 cu ft | 32,000 grain | One 1 cu ft bag |
| 10 x 54 | 1.5 cu ft | 48,000 grain | A 1.5 cu ft kit, or two 1 cu ft bags |
| 12 x 48 | 2.0 cu ft | 64,000 grain | Two 1 cu ft bags, or a 2 cu ft pack |
| 13 x 54 | 2.5 cu ft | 80,000 grain | A 2.5 cu ft kit, or three 1 cu ft bags |

*Common pairings, not a standard. Pentair's whole-house softener spec sheet, for example, lists 10x44, 10x54 and 12x48 resin tanks. Check your own unit's figure before you order.*

**Leave the headspace empty.** A filled tank has resin to roughly two-thirds of its height, with the top third left as freeboard. During backwash the bed lifts into that space. Overfilling packs the bed against the top screen, and backwash can then carry beads to the drain.

**The 1.5 cubic foot trap.** The single most common softener size takes 1.5 cubic feet, and most branded resin comes in 1 cubic foot bags. Two bags means paying for half a cubic foot you will not use. That is why a 1.5 cubic foot kit can beat a cheaper-per-bag resin on the total bill.

**Gravel underbedding.** Some tanks have a layer of gravel under the resin. If yours did, clean gravel can usually go back in. AFWFilters' kit page says gravel is optional for standard softeners, but follow the valve and tank maker's instruction if it differs.

## Fine Mesh Resin: Why It Is Not on This List

We found no fine mesh resin pack confirmed on Amazon on the day we checked. Fine mesh is mostly sold inside complete well-water softeners, or from specialist sellers' own stores. AFWFilters, for example, lists fine mesh refill kits in 1, 1.5 and 2 cubic foot sizes.

Fine mesh only helps when the iron is dissolved and stays dissolved until it reaches the bed. It also needs a lower drain line flow control rate than standard resin in the same tank, or the backwash lifts the small beads out to the drain. If your well passes the conditions set out in [standard vs fine mesh resin](/blog/standard-vs-fine-mesh-resin/), buy fine mesh from a seller who will also supply the matching flow control.

## Before You Order

1. **Confirm the resin is the problem.** A softener that stops softening because it no longer draws brine has a valve or brine-side fault, not worn resin. Work through [why a water softener is not using salt](/blog/water-softener-not-using-salt/) first. New resin will not fix a blocked injector.
2. **Check the bed.** Fine amber beads at the taps point to a broken riser or distributor, which needs replacing along with the resin. Mushy, swollen beads that squash between your fingers are chlorine-oxidised. Resin coated brown or orange is iron-fouled.
3. **Plan the protection.** If chlorine wore out the old bed, the new one meets the same water. Moving to 10 percent helps. Above about 2 mg/L, or on chloramine, put carbon ahead of the softener, and on chloramine make it catalytic carbon.
4. **Reprogram after the rebed.** Set the capacity for the resin volume and the salt dose you actually use, not the old box rating.

## Who Should Buy None of These

- **Owners whose softener is not drawing brine.** The resin is probably fine. Fix the brine side.
- **Valves past about twelve years, or with a fault history.** New resin under a head that may fail next is poor value. Price a valve replacement or a new unit first.
- **Wells with rust particles, manganese or iron bacteria.** No resin grade survives fouling. An iron filter goes in first.
- **Chloramine supplies with no plan for carbon.** Even 10 percent resin will be oxidised again. Budget for catalytic carbon ahead of the softener, or accept a shorter bed life.
- **Cabinet softeners with sealed resin cartridges.** Some all-in-one units are not designed to be rebedded. Check the manual before buying loose resin.

For most city homes, the answer is a 10 percent crosslink resin bought in exactly the volume the tank holds. For a 1.5 cubic foot tank that means a kit, not two bags. For a clean well, standard resin is enough. And whatever wore out the last bed is still in the water, so deal with it before you pour in new resin.
