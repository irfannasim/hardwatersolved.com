**In the Fleck 5600SXT vs 7000SXT choice, the 7000SXT only earns its place in a house whose busiest moment runs past about 12 to 15 gpm, roughly five or six fixtures at once.** Pentair rates the 3/4 inch 5600SXT at 20 gpm and the 1-1/4 inch 7000SXT at 28 gpm, both at a 15 psi drop, so the bigger valve loses about half the pressure at any flow. But the 5600SXT is rated only for softener tanks up to 12 inches, and its meter's stated accuracy ends at 15 gpm. That, not the valve body, is where it becomes the bottleneck. Below that, a 7000SXT on the same tank buys nothing you will feel. And on 2 October 2026, no complete 7000SXT system was for sale on Amazon.

## Fleck 5600SXT vs 7000SXT: The Spec Sheets Side by Side

Both valves are Pentair Fleck piston valves with the same SXT controller, so the programming, display and regeneration modes are almost identical. Everything that differs is about size.

| | Fleck 5600SXT | Fleck 7000SXT |
|---|---|---|
| Valve body | Fiber-reinforced polymer | Fiber-reinforced polymer |
| Inlet and outlet | 3/4, 1 or 1-1/4 in | 3/4, 1, 1-1/4 or 1-1/2 in |
| Continuous flow, 15 psi drop | 20 gpm | 28 gpm (standard piston) |
| Peak flow, 25 psi drop | 26 gpm | 36 gpm (standard piston) |
| Cv, flow at 1 psi drop | 5.0 | 7.1 |
| Maximum backwash, 25 psi drop | 7 gpm | 7 gpm standard; 16 gpm high-flow piston |
| Softener tank diameter | 6 to 12 in | 8 to 24 in |
| Distributor pilot | 0.8125 or 1.05 in | 1.05 in (32 mm) |
| Regeneration | Downflow or upflow | Downflow only |
| Meter | 3/4 in turbine, 0.25 to 15 gpm, ±5% | 1-1/4 in turbine, 2 to 40 gpm, +5/−8% |
| Drain line | 1/2 in NPTF | 3/4 or 1 in NPTM |
| Height above tank | 7.5 in | 9 in |
| Working pressure | 20 to 125 psi | 20 to 125 psi |
| Water temperature | 34 to 110°F | 34 to 110°F |
| Cycle time range (SXT) | 0 to 199 min per cycle | 0 to 199 min per cycle |
| Valve weight, metered | 7 lb | 11 lb |

*All flows are for the valve alone at a 50 psi inlet, from Pentair's sheets.*

Two lines are easy to miss. The 7000SXT also comes with a **high-flow piston**, rated at 36 gpm at 15 psi and 16 gpm of backwash, which is what lets it serve the widest tanks. And the 7000SXT's meter is only rated for accuracy **from 2 gpm upward**, against 0.25 gpm for the 5600SXT. In a small house where water is often drawn at a trickle, the bigger valve's meter is outside its stated range more of the time.

## Pressure Drop vs Flow on One Chart

Pressure loss through a valve rises with the square of the flow. Double the flow and the drop roughly quadruples. Fitting that curve through Pentair's rated points gives an equivalent Cv of about 5.2 for the 5600SXT and 7.2 for the 7000SXT, close to the sheets' own 5.0 and 7.1.

| Flow | Typical load | 5600SXT valve | 7000SXT valve | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 gpm | 2 showers | 0.9 psi | 0.5 psi | 0.4 psi |
| 10 gpm | 4 fixtures | 3.7 psi | 1.9 psi | 1.8 psi |
| 12 gpm | 5 fixtures | 5.3 psi | 2.8 psi | 2.5 psi |
| 15 gpm | 6 fixtures | 8.3 psi | 4.3 psi | 4.0 psi |
| 20 gpm | 8 fixtures | 14.8 psi | 7.7 psi | 7.1 psi |
| 25 gpm | 10 fixtures | 23.1 psi | 12.1 psi | 11.0 psi |

*Illustrative, valve body only, fixtures counted at 2.5 gpm each. A complete softener adds the loss through its resin bed, distributor and bypass.*

At four fixtures the gap is under 2 psi, which nobody notices. At six it is 4 psi on the valve alone, on top of what the bed costs. That is noticeable in an upstairs shower on a 40 psi supply. How to add up a whole pressure budget, and what counts as normal, is in [water softener pressure drop](/blog/water-softener-pressure-drop/).

## The Fixture Count Where the 5600SXT Becomes the Bottleneck

The valve body is not what usually limits a 5600SXT system. Three published limits converge at the same point, and each one comes from Pentair's own sheet or a standard design rule.

**1. The tank it can serve.** Pentair's sheet says the 5600SXT's backwash capability accommodates softener tanks up to 12 inches, and lists 6 to 12 inches as its softener range. A resin bed carries flow by its cross-section. At the peak design rate of 15 gpm per square foot, the widest tank the 5600SXT serves gives:

| Tank | Bed area | Peak at 15 gpm/sq ft | Valve that can serve it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 in | 0.55 sq ft | 8.2 gpm | 5600SXT or 7000SXT |
| 12 in | 0.79 sq ft | 11.8 gpm | 5600SXT or 7000SXT |
| 13 in | 0.92 sq ft | 13.9 gpm | 7000SXT |
| 14 in | 1.07 sq ft | 16.1 gpm | 7000SXT |
| 16 in | 1.40 sq ft | 21.0 gpm | 7000SXT |

**2. Its meter.** The 5600SXT's 3/4 inch turbine meter is rated for accuracy from 0.25 to 15 gpm. Above 15 gpm, the gallons it counts, and so the timing of each regeneration, are outside its stated accuracy.

**3. Its own pressure loss.** At 15 gpm the valve alone takes about 8 psi, twice the 7000SXT's figure.

Put together: **at about 12 gpm, or five fixtures, a 5600SXT system on its largest tank reaches the bed's peak design rate. At 15 gpm, or six fixtures, it reaches the end of its meter's range.** A house whose busiest moment regularly sits there needs a wider tank, and a wider tank needs a 7000SXT.

### Counting your own fixtures

Count what actually runs together on your worst morning, not what is plumbed in. The EPA notes that standard showerheads use 2.5 gpm and WaterSense models no more than 2.0. A washing machine filling, a kitchen tap and a running tub filler each add their own flow. Time any fixture you are unsure of by filling a 1-gallon container: 20 seconds is 3 gpm.

For a code-based figure, the fixture-unit method in [grain capacity vs peak flow rate](/blog/grain-capacity-vs-peak-flow-rate/) works out a 2.5-bathroom house at about 17 gpm and a four-bathroom house at about 20 gpm. Those are upper bounds; a practical count for the same 2.5-bathroom house is often nearer 8 to 9 gpm.

| Your busiest moment | Practical example | Valve |
|---|---|---|
| Under 8 gpm | 2 showers and a tap | 5600SXT on a 10 in tank |
| 8 to 12 gpm | 3 showers, or 2 showers and laundry | 5600SXT on a 12 in tank |
| 12 to 16 gpm | 4 showers and laundry, or a tub filler running | 7000SXT on a 13 or 14 in tank |
| Over 16 gpm | Large home, multi-head showers, irrigation on the soft loop | 7000SXT on 16 in or wider, or two tanks in parallel |

## When the 7000SXT Is Wasted

A bigger valve on a narrow tank does not add flow. The resin bed still sets the ceiling. The 48,000-grain 7000SXT system listed on Amazon used a 10 x 54 inch tank with 1.5 cubic feet of resin, and the 64,000-grain listing a 12 x 52 inch tank with 2.0 cubic feet. Both tanks are inside the 5600SXT's own range, so the 7000SXT valve there:

- passes the same water as a 5600SXT would, because the bed carries about 8 to 12 gpm at peak;
- saves a few psi at high flow, which only helps on a weak supply;
- meters less accurately at very low flow, from its 2 gpm floor.

The 7000SXT pays off when it is paired with the tank it was designed for, 13 inches and up. The 80,000-grain listing, with 2.5 cubic feet of resin, 1.25 inch connections and an 18 x 33 inch brine tank, is the shape of system the valve makes sense on. A 2.5 cubic foot bed normally sits in a 13 or 14 inch tank.

The opposite mistake is just as common: a large house on a 5600SXT with a 64,000-grain bed, chosen for capacity. Grain capacity sets how many days between regenerations. It does not change flow.

The hidden costs are around the valve, not in it:

- **A wider tank holds more resin, and more resin needs more salt per regeneration.** At an efficient 6 lb per cubic foot, a 2.5 cubic foot bed uses about 15 lb a regeneration against 9 lb for 1.5 cubic feet. It also regenerates less often, so annual salt use depends on your hardness and water use, not the valve.
- **A wider tank backwashes at a higher rate.** The drain line flow control is sized to the tank diameter, so each backwash sends more water to drain, and the drain itself must take it.
- **The plumbing must be sized to match.** A 1-1/4 inch valve on a 3/4 inch loop throws most of its advantage away. The [low water pressure after a water softener](/blog/low-water-pressure-after-water-softener/) guide shows how much the connecting pipe can cost.

For context, a complete 5600SXT 64,000-grain system is about $100 more than the 48,000-grain one from the same seller. Budget for a 7000SXT system on a 13 or 14 inch tank to cost meaningfully more than that, and get a written quote that names the tank size, resin volume and piston type.

## Who Should Buy Neither

- **Homes on a low-yield well.** If the pump delivers 8 gpm, no valve will pass 15. Size to the pump and the pressure tank, not the fixtures.
- **Houses needing soft water during regeneration.** Both are single-tank valves; the house gets hard water while they regenerate. A twin-tank alternating system suits round-the-clock use.
- **Wells with iron, manganese or sulfur above a softener's tolerance.** Treat the water first; the valve size is irrelevant to that problem.
- **Anyone who wants app control.** The SXT controller on both valves has none.

The verdict on Fleck 5600SXT vs 7000SXT: buy the 5600SXT for any home whose busiest moment stays under about 12 gpm, and spend the difference on the right resin. Move to the 7000SXT only when the house needs a tank wider than 12 inches, and buy the tank and the valve as one sized system, not the valve on its own.
