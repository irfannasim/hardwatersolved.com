**This WaterBoss water softener review tests one claim: that a compact cabinet softener also takes care of iron and sediment.** It does, inside a narrower window than the box suggests. The WaterBoss 900's manual rates it for up to 10 ppm of iron, but only clear-water (ferrous) iron, at pH 7 or above, and only while your hardness plus 5 grains per gallon for every ppm of iron stays under 90. Its filter layer handles fine dirt, not sand. Above 5 ppm of iron, with any iron that has already oxidised, or with sand in the water, a separate filter is still needed.

Inside that window, it is one of the most salt-efficient cabinet softeners on paper, at 5,510 grains per pound of salt.

The Model 900 is the one most buyers mean. Home Depot sells it as a 36,400-grain unit with a 10 gpm rated flow at a 15 psi drop. The Model 700 is the smaller 22,000-grain cabinet.

## The Iron Claim, Tested Against the Manual's Limits

"Reduces up to 10 ppm of iron" is true, and it is the least informative part of the specification. Four conditions in the manual decide whether it applies to your water.

![Chart of the WaterBoss 900 water softener operating envelope: hardness plus 5 gpg per ppm of iron must stay under 90, iron under 10 ppm, with 5 ppm marked as the guideline](/diagrams/waterboss-water-softener-iron-hardness-operating-envelope.svg "The 10 ppm rating only applies at low hardness; the safe zone ends at 5 ppm")

**1. The iron must be ferrous.** The manual's iron table is blunt. Ferrous iron is the "only type of iron that can be treated with a water softener". Ferric iron "can eventually foul a resin bed" and should be filtered out first. Colloidal iron "a softener cannot remove", and organic or bacterial iron needs additional treatment. If water comes out of the tap already orange or cloudy, the 10 ppm figure does not apply at all. A half-hour glass test, described in [dissolved vs oxidized iron in well water](/blog/dissolved-vs-oxidized-iron-in-well-water/), tells you which kind you have.

**2. The pH must be 7 or higher.** Below that, WaterBoss points you to its acid-neutralising filter first.

**3. Hardness and iron share one limit.** The 90 gpg rating is for compensated hardness: your raw hardness plus 5 gpg for every ppm of ferrous iron. At 10 ppm of iron, that leaves room for only 40 gpg of hardness. A well at 45 gpg and 10 ppm sits inside the iron rating and still fails the specification.

**4. Five ppm is the realistic ceiling.** The manual itself notes that Wisconsin limits iron reduction claims to 5 ppm. [Penn State Extension's guide to iron and manganese](https://extension.psu.edu/iron-and-manganese-in-private-water-systems) recommends a softener for iron only when dissolved iron is below 5 mg/L, and only when the raw water has not met air or chlorine first. Between 5 and 10 ppm the WaterBoss is working inside its own rating but outside the independent guideline.

### What heavy iron does to salt and regeneration

The iron-sizing rule turns every ppm of iron into extra hardness load, and the arithmetic is laid out in [how iron changes water softener sizing](/blog/how-iron-changes-water-softener-sizing/). Here it is for the WaterBoss 900, a family of four using 280 gallons a day on 15 gpg water, at the 8.5 lb dose (29,842 grains):

| Ferrous iron | Compensated hardness | Grains a day | Days between regenerations | Salt a year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 ppm | 15 gpg | 4,200 | 7.1 | about 440 lb |
| 2 ppm | 25 gpg | 7,000 | 4.3 | about 730 lb |
| 5 ppm | 40 gpg | 11,200 | 2.7 | about 1,160 lb |
| 10 ppm | 65 gpg | 18,200 | 1.6 | about 1,890 lb |

*Calculated from the manual's capacity table, not measured, and before reserve.*

On top of this, the manual asks iron users to run the powerClean cycle every six months. It regenerates every other day with 5 lb of salt for at least two weeks, which is roughly 70 lb of extra salt a year. It also recommends a resin cleaner, or salt with an iron-cleaning additive, every six months.

The table shows the economic limit as clearly as the specification shows the technical one. At 5 ppm the softener uses about two and a half times the salt it would on hardness alone and regenerates every two to three days. That is the point where an iron filter ahead of the softener usually earns its place. The trade-off is costed in [water softener vs iron filter](/blog/water-softener-vs-iron-filter/).

## The Sediment Claim, Tested

![Neglected sink with a rusty faucet, the stained fixtures that iron and dirt in untreated well water leave behind](https://images.pexels.com/photos/19642046/pexels-photo-19642046.jpeg)

Retailers sell the 900 as reducing "dirt and sediment". The manual supports a narrower version of that. The 700 and 900 hold a layer of self-cleaning filter media that the backwash flushes during regeneration, and the installation section says the softener can treat a combination of constituents including iron, dirt and sediment. The same manual adds three limits:

- **No sand.** "If dirt, sand, or large particles are present in the water supply, the appropriate WaterBoss filter can eliminate this problem."
- **No unusual water.** If the supply contains sand, sulfur, bacteria, iron bacteria, tannins, algae, oil or acid, it says to pre-treat before the softener.
- **The warranty agrees.** It excludes water containing "sand, bacterial iron, algae, sulphur, tannins, organic matter, or other unusual substances" unless the specification says the unit handles them.

Some retailer listings quote a micron rating for the filter layer; the manual we read does not give one. Treat the filter layer as protection against fine dirt, not as a replacement for a sediment filter on a well that pumps sand. Where a spin-down or cartridge filter goes, and why, is covered in [sediment prefilter before a softener](/blog/sediment-prefilter-before-a-softener/).

## Where a Separate Filter Is Still Needed

Put a filter ahead of a WaterBoss when any of these is true:

1. **Ferric, colloidal or bacterial iron** is present, whatever the ppm.
2. **Ferrous iron is above 5 ppm**, or hardness plus 5 × iron is above 90 gpg on the 900 or 70 on the 700.
3. **The pH is below 7.**
4. **The well produces sand** or coarse particles.
5. **The water is chlorinated**, in which case the 950 or a carbon stage is the better plan.

Everything else, including a typical rural well at 15 to 25 gpg with 1 to 3 ppm of clear-water iron, is the WaterBoss 900's home ground.

## Warranty and Ownership

The owner's manual we read gives three years on mechanical and electronic parts and ten years on the media tank and valve body and the brine cabinet. Media is not warranted. Labour and service calls are not covered, defective parts go back to WaterBoss prepaid, and the warranty card has to be returned within 30 days of installation. Retailer listings for the current Model 900 advertise five years on parts, so check the card in your box.

Day to day, the 900 is a 26-minute regeneration demand softener with a salt cabinet of about 160 to 170 lb, and it fits where a two-tank unit will not.

## Who Should Not Buy a WaterBoss

- **Wells with more than 5 ppm of iron**, or any iron that has already oxidised. Filter first, or choose a dedicated iron system.
- **Very hard water with iron.** The compensated limit runs out fast: 60 gpg plus 6 ppm is already 90.
- **Chlorinated city water**, if you were looking at the 700 or 900. The 950 is WaterBoss's own city model.
- **Large homes with high peak demand.** One cabinet and a 1-inch connection have limits that a twin-tank system does not.
- **Anyone hoping to avoid salt.** With heavy iron, a WaterBoss uses more of it, not less. The alternatives are compared in [salt-based vs salt-free water treatment](/blog/salt-based-vs-salt-free-water-treatment/), but salt-free systems do not remove iron either.

The WaterBoss 900 is a good compact softener for moderate well water with modest clear-water iron. Read its iron rating as a ceiling, not a target.
