**The Fleck 9100SXT is a twin-tank softener valve that keeps one resin tank in service while the other regenerates or waits. That gives soft water around the clock and lets each tank run to exhaustion, with no day of reserve held back.** It does not double flow, because only one tank is in service at a time. On a typical family's water, a single Fleck 5600SXT of the same resin volume costs less up front and less over ten years. The 9100SXT pays for itself only when the daily hardness load is heavy, as in a large household on very hard water, or when water is used around the clock.

This Fleck 9100SXT review works from Pentair's technical sheet and SXT manual, the sellers' Amazon listings and the AFWFilters store. The capacity and cost figures are worked examples. Nothing here comes from physical testing.

## How the 9100SXT Alternates Its Tanks

![Three-step diagram of a Fleck 9100SXT twin-tank softener: tank A in service with B on standby, the switch to B while A regenerates with soft water and brine, then B in service with A on standby](/diagrams/fleck-9100sxt-twin-tank-alternation-cycle.svg "Each tank runs until its capacity is used; the other takes over at once")

Pentair describes the 9100 as providing "continuous soft water provision with only one tank in use, other being in regeneration or stand-by," with "regeneration with softened water." In practice:

1. **Tank A softens the supply.** The turbine meter counts gallons.
2. **When A's programmed capacity is used, the valve switches to B immediately**, at any time of day, and regenerates A. The backwash and rinse water for A comes from B, so A is regenerated with soft water.
3. **B softens; A waits, fully regenerated.** The cycle repeats.

Two consequences follow. There is no 2 a.m. regeneration window, because regeneration happens whenever a tank is exhausted. And because the switch is immediate, the system does not need to keep a day of capacity in reserve. The full logic of reserve on single-tank units is set out in [water softener reserve capacity](/blog/water-softener-reserve-capacity/).

## Fleck 9100SXT Review of the Capacity Math

This is where most 9100SXT owners go wrong, in two different ways.

### Mistake 1: entering both tanks' capacity

Listings often advertise the combined figure. One CAI Technologies listing describes "Twin tank 96,000 grain total system capacity" for two 1.5 cubic foot tanks. But the valve regenerates one tank at a time. The unit capacity you enter in the controller is the capacity of **one tank**.

### Mistake 2: entering the box rating

The 48,000 grains per tank is the maximum-salt figure. At an efficient 6 lb of salt per cubic foot, each 1.5 cubic foot tank delivers about 31,500 grains. That figure is explained, with the full table, in [advertised vs usable softener capacity](/blog/advertised-vs-usable-softener-capacity/).

| Resin per tank | Sold as | Capacity per tank at 6 lb/cu ft | Salt per regeneration | Total sold as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 cu ft | 32,000 per tank | about 21,000 | 6 lb | 64,000 |
| 1.5 cu ft | 48,000 per tank | about 31,500 | 9 lb | 96,000 |
| 2.0 cu ft | 64,000 per tank | about 42,000 | 12 lb | 128,000 |

### The reserve: small, not zero

Twin-tank marketing often says "no reserve needed." Pentair's 5600SXT service manual, which covers the SXT controller's two-tank setting, is more precise: "in twin tank systems with soft-water regeneration, the reserve capacity should be set to the volume of water used during regeneration to prevent hard water break-through." Regenerating tank A uses soft water from tank B, and that water counts against B's capacity.

Most residential softeners use roughly 40 to 100 gallons per regeneration, as covered in [how much water regeneration uses](/blog/how-much-water-does-regeneration-use/). Measure or estimate your system's figure, then set the reserve to it.

### Worked example

A family of four uses about 300 gallons a day on 15 gpg water: 4,500 grains of hardness a day. On a 1.5 cubic foot per tank 9100SXT:

| Setting | Value | Working |
|---|---|---|
| Unit capacity | 31,500 grains | One tank at 6 lb/cu ft |
| Hardness | 15 gpg | Add about 4 gpg per ppm of iron if present |
| Gallons per tank | 2,100 | 31,500 ÷ 15 |
| Reserve | 50 gallons | About one regeneration's water (assumed) |
| Gallons before switching | 2,050 | 2,100 − 50 |
| Days per tank | about 6.8 | 2,050 ÷ 300 |

A single 5600SXT with the same 1.5 cubic feet of resin has to hold back a day of reserve, 300 gallons, so it delivers about 1,800 gallons per cycle, around 6 days. The twin gets about 14% more softening out of each regeneration. The step-by-step method for both valve types is in [how to set softener capacity and reserve](/blog/how-to-set-softener-capacity-and-reserve/).

## Flow and Pressure Drop

A twin-tank softener does not double your flow. Only one tank is in service, so the house sees one resin bed's resistance plus the valve's.

| Source | What it says |
|---|---|
| Pentair 9100 technical sheet, valve alone, 1 inch | About 4.5 m³/h (roughly 20 gpm) at a 1 bar drop; about 5.9 m³/h (roughly 26 gpm) at 1.8 bar |
| Pentair 9100 technical sheet, valve alone, 3/4 inch | About 4 m³/h (roughly 18 gpm) at a 1 bar drop |
| AFWFilters 9100SXT, 1.5 cu ft per tank | 6.5 gpm service, 9.8 gpm peak |

The AFWFilters figure is much lower than the valve alone and lower than many single-tank 48,000-grain listings claim. It most likely reflects the 1.5 cubic foot bed in a 10-inch tank, which limits flow far more than the valve does. The figure is conservative, and the same bed limit applies to a single tank with the same resin. If your home regularly runs several showers at once, size each tank for the flow, not just the hardness. The relationship is explained in [water softener pressure drop](/blog/water-softener-pressure-drop/).

To check your own installation, fit pressure gauges before and after the softener and open several taps. A drop well above 15 psi at your normal peak is a sign the bed is undersized for your flow.

## Cost Against a Single 5600SXT of Equal Capacity

The comparison that matters: the same resin per regeneration, single tank vs twin. The 9100SXT costs noticeably more up front than a single 5600SXT with the same 1.5 cubic feet of resin. Its saving is salt, because it never regenerates a bed with a day's reserve still unused.

*Assumptions: 6 lb of salt per cubic foot; single-tank reserve of one day's hardness load; twin reserve of 50 gallons of regeneration water; salt at $7 per 40 lb bag; water, electricity and resin replacement not included. Add the current price of each system to compare total cost.*

What the numbers say:

- **On a typical family's water, the twin does not pay back.** It saves about $12 a year in salt, roughly $117 over ten years, far less than the twin's higher purchase price.
- **On a heavy load, it does.** For six people on 25 gpg water, a single 1.5 cubic foot tank holds back over a third of its capacity as reserve every cycle. The twin uses its whole bed and saves about $1,056 in salt over ten years against that tank, and about $666 against a larger 2.0 cubic foot single tank. When this review was written, both savings were larger than the twin's extra purchase price, so the twin came out cheaper over ten years. Check today's prices against those savings.
- **The real reason to buy a twin is usually not salt.** It is round-the-clock soft water: homes with night shifts, small businesses, or any use pattern where a 2 a.m. regeneration window is a problem. When a single tank does run out of soft water, the cause is usually undersizing or a bad reserve setting rather than the single-tank design, as covered in [single-tank vs twin-tank water softeners](/blog/single-tank-vs-twin-tank-water-softeners/).

## Who Should Not Buy a Fleck 9100SXT

- **A typical family on moderately hard water.** A correctly programmed single 5600SXT does the same job for less.
- **Anyone hoping to double their flow rate.** One tank serves the house at a time.
- **Homes short on floor space.** Two mineral tanks plus a brine tank need more room than a single unit.
- **Anyone who will enter the box rating or the combined total as the capacity.** The system will run past exhaustion or regenerate wastefully. Use the per-tank usable figure.
- **Buyers who mainly want lower salt bills on light use.** The saving is real only when the daily load is large relative to one tank.
- **Anyone unsure whether they need salt-based softening at all.** That decision comes first, and is covered in [salt-based vs salt-free water treatment](/blog/salt-based-vs-salt-free-water-treatment/).

The verdict of this Fleck 9100SXT review: it is a well-proven twin-tank valve with the same serviceable Fleck parts and SXT controls as the 5600SXT. Buy it for a heavy daily load, very hard water or round-the-clock use, and program it with one tank's usable capacity and a reserve equal to the regeneration water. For an ordinary household, the single-tank 5600SXT is the better value.
