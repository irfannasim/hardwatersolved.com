## Sand, Silt or Cloudiness: Match the Micron Rating to the Particle

![Best sediment filters for well water compared on a micron chart: 90 µm screen and 50 µm spin-down catch sand, 20 µm Filter-Ag catches silt, 5 µm cartridge catches fine silt](/diagrams/sediment-filters-for-well-water-micron-staging-chart.svg "Coarse stages protect fine ones, so stage sediment filters from large to small")

Sediment is sorted by grain size on the standard geology scale: **sand** is coarser than about 63 microns, **silt** runs from about 4 to 63 microns, and **clay** is finer than about 4 microns. Clay and very fine silt are what make well water look cloudy. A filter's micron rating is the size it is built to stop. Particles larger than that are caught, and smaller ones pass.

That gives three practical rules:

1. **The finest filter you need is set by the finest particle that causes a problem,** not by the smallest number you can buy. Sand that scores valve seals is stopped at 50 microns. Cloudiness needs 20 microns or finer.
2. **A fine filter on its own clogs.** A 5 micron cartridge on a sandy well fills with sand that a 50 micron screen would have flushed away for free. That is the reason to stage coarse to fine.
3. **Ratings are not all measured the same way.** A screen's rating is its mesh opening. A media bed or a cartridge is rated by what it catches as water passes through depth. Compare them as rough bands, not to the last micron.

The [VIQUA UV owner's manual](https://viqua.com/wp-content/uploads/MNL520585_RevB.pdf) puts this into practice. It requires a 5 micron sediment filter ahead of its UV chamber. Where sediment keeps getting through, it suggests stepped filtration: a 20 micron filter followed by a 5 micron filter, then the UV.

## The Cartridge Stage: Last in Line, Not First

A 20-inch "Big Blue" housing with a pleated or spun 5 micron cartridge is the most common sediment filter on a well, and the most misused. It is a polishing stage. Placed first on a sandy well, it fills within days.

It earns its place in two situations:

- **Ahead of UV.** VIQUA's manual requires a 5 micron filter before its chamber.
- **As a final stage after a coarse screen,** where some fine silt still gets through to fixtures or a softener valve.

**Running cost is the catch.** Fresh water supplier ESP lists a Pentair DGD-5005-20 5 micron 4.5 x 20-inch cartridge at $48.99, and advises replacing well cartridges every 1 to 6 months, never longer than 12. That is about $98 a year at two changes and up to $588 at twelve. A coarse stage ahead of it is what keeps the number at the low end. How to read the pressure drop and time each change is covered in [when to replace a sediment prefilter before a softener](/blog/sediment-prefilter-before-a-softener/).

### A worked example: one cartridge, or a spin-down plus a cartridge

Take a well that sheds sand and a little fine silt, feeding a UV system that needs a 5 micron filter in front of it. There are two ways to set it up.

| | 5 µm cartridge alone | WSP50ARB spin-down, then 5 µm cartridge |
|---|---|---|
| Up-front, beyond the housing | $0 | about $198 |
| Cartridge changes a year (illustrative) | 12, because sand loads it | 2, because the screen takes the sand |
| Cartridge cost a year at $48.99 | about $588 | about $98 |
| First-year total | about $588 | about $296 |
| Each later year | about $588 | about $98 |

*Illustrative. The number of changes depends on how much sand and silt your well carries. Use the pressure drop across the housing, not the calendar, to time each change.*

On these assumptions the spin-down pays for itself in the first year and saves about $490 a year after that. The bigger benefit is harder to price. A cartridge that is loading up fast drops pressure at the taps and at the UV between changes. A cartridge that only sees fine silt holds steady.

The same logic applies ahead of a softener. Sand that reaches a control valve wears its seals and pistons, and a repair costs more than any filter on this page. A coarse screen is the cheapest protection a well system can have.

## Which Sediment Filter for Your Well

- **Sand in aerators, toilet tanks or a softener valve:** a spin-down. The WSP50ARB for most homes, the SEDFLUSH for high flow.
- **Cloudy water from a well with sand as well:** a spin-down first, then a Filter-Ag tank if the house's flow fits, or a 5 micron cartridge if it does not.
- **Clear water feeding a UV system:** a 5 micron cartridge immediately ahead of the UV, as the maker requires.
- **Specks in water after a softener:** check what they are before buying anything. Black or amber grains can be resin from a failing softener rather than well sediment, and a filter ahead of the softener will not stop them. [Black specks in water after a softener](/blog/black-specks-in-water-after-softener/) explains how to tell them apart.

Placement matters as much as the filter. The coarse stage normally goes right after the pressure tank. If the well pumps enough sand to wear the pressure switch, a spin-down can go ahead of the tank instead.

## Who Should Buy None of These

- **Wells with dissolved iron and no sediment.** Clear water that turns orange after standing is dissolved iron. No sediment filter stops it, and a fine one clogs with whatever oxidises in the housing. An iron filter is the purchase.
- **Wells that suddenly start pumping sand.** A new sand problem usually means a worn pump, a failing screen or a drop in water level. A filter treats the symptom while the pump wears.
- **Muddy water after heavy rain.** That points to surface water reaching the well, which is a bacteria risk as much as a sediment one. Test for coliform bacteria and have the wellhead inspected first.
- **Homes on low-yield wells considering a backwashing tank.** Without the backwash flow, it cannot clean itself; choose a screen or cartridge.

Choose the best sediment filter for well water by the smallest particle that actually causes trouble, and put a coarse, self-cleaning stage ahead of anything fine. For most wells that means one spin-down. For some it means a spin-down plus one finer stage, and almost never a cartridge alone.
