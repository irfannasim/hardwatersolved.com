The **best UV systems for well water** are the VIQUA VH410M, with a UV sensor, at about $1,310, and the VIQUA VH410 without it at about $880. Both treat 14 gpm at the 40 mJ/cm² dose NSF and EPA use. The iSpring UVF55FS, a 12 gpm unit, is a budget option with no published dose. Any of them only works on water that is already clear. VIQUA's manual sets the limits at the UV: iron under 0.3 ppm, manganese under 0.05 ppm, hardness under 7 gpg, turbidity under 1 NTU and UV transmittance above 75%. It also requires a 5 micron filter in front. On most wells that means the UV comes last, after an iron filter, a softener and a sediment filter.

## What Must Come First: The Pretreatment Limits

![Best UV systems for well water need pretreatment: sediment screen, iron and manganese filter, softener and 5 micron cartridge ahead of the UV, each delivering a VIQUA limit](/diagrams/uv-system-well-water-pretreatment-limits-by-stage.svg "Every stage ahead of the UV exists to meet one of the limits in its manual")

UV light inactivates microorganisms only when it reaches them. Iron, manganese and hardness coat the quartz sleeve around the lamp. Turbidity and tannins shade microbes from the light. The [VIQUA VH410 owner's manual](https://viqua.com/wp-content/uploads/MNL520585_RevB.pdf) lists the water it expects at the chamber:

| Parameter | VIQUA's limit at the UV | Stage that usually meets it |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | under 0.3 ppm | Iron filter, or a fine-mesh softener at low levels |
| Manganese | under 0.05 ppm | Iron and manganese filter |
| Hardness | under 7 gpg; "if total hardness exceeds 7 gpg, the water should be softened" | Softener |
| Turbidity | under 1 NTU | Sediment filtration, ending with 5 µm |
| Tannins | under 0.1 ppm | A dedicated tannin stage |
| UV transmittance | over 75%; "call factory" below that | All of the above |

Two more installation rules come from the same manual. "A 5-micron sediment filter must precede the UV System," and "the UV System should be the final piece of treatment equipment." Where sediment keeps getting through, VIQUA suggests a 20 micron filter followed by a 5 micron filter, then the UV.

Read your lab report against that table before choosing a model. A well with 2 ppm of iron and 15 gpg of hardness needs an iron filter and a softener ahead of any UV on this page. Buying the UV first, and the pretreatment later, means a UV that is not doing its job in the meantime.

**iSpring does not publish equivalent limits** for the UVF55FS in anything we could open. Treat VIQUA's figures as the minimum for any UV. A sleeve coats the same way whatever brand surrounds it.

## Flow Depends on the Dose You Choose

UV systems are sold on a headline flow, and the VH410 is advertised at 18 gpm. VIQUA's spec sheet shows that figure is for one dose:

| VH410 / VH410M flow | UV dose | What the dose is |
|---|---|---|
| 34 gpm | 16 mJ/cm² | US Public Health figure |
| 18 gpm | 30 mJ/cm² | VIQUA Standard |
| 14 gpm | 40 mJ/cm² | NSF/EPA figure |

*All at 95% UV transmittance and end of lamp life, per VIQUA.*

The higher the flow, the less time water spends in the light, and the lower the dose. For a well, size on the **40 mJ/cm² figure**. That is the dose NSF uses for Class A systems, the class certified to treat contaminated water. A home whose busiest moment passes 14 gpm needs a larger chamber, not the 18 gpm label.

Transmittance matters as much as flow. VIQUA's ratings assume 95% UVT, water that lets 95% of UV light through a centimetre. Well water that only reaches 75% delivers a lower dose at the same flow. That is why the pretreatment stages are not optional.

## Installation Details That Decide Whether It Protects the House

A correctly sized UV can still leave water untreated if it is plumbed or wired carelessly. VIQUA's manual sets out the points that matter, and they apply to any brand:

- **Cold line, before any branch.** The manual says to install the UV "on cold water line only, before any branched lines". An outdoor tap or a water heater feed teed off ahead of it receives water that never passed the lamp.
- **Last in the train, after the 5 micron filter.** Anything installed after the UV, such as a carbon bed or a softener, can grow bacteria back into treated water.
- **A GFCI outlet, and a surge protector.** VIQUA requires a ground fault circuit interrupter and strongly recommends a surge protective device for the controller. A tripped GFCI that nobody notices is a UV that is off, which is another argument for a sensor model with an alarm.
- **Room to pull the lamp.** Leave clearance above or beside the chamber equal to its length, so the lamp and sleeve can be withdrawn without cutting pipe.
- **Disinfect at start-up.** The whole system after the UV, including the pressure and water heater tanks, should be sterilised with chlorine before start-up, according to VIQUA. Otherwise bacteria already in the plumbing remain there. On a well with high iron or manganese, chlorine in the water heater oxidises what has built up, so expect repeated flushing.
- **Hot water at the chamber.** During long periods with no flow, VIQUA warns the water in the chamber can reach about 60 °C (140 °F). Run the tap to purge it, or fit a temperature management valve at the outlet.

None of these costs much at installation. Most are expensive to fix afterwards, especially an outdoor line already teed off ahead of the chamber.

## When UV Is Not the Answer

UV treats water passing through it at that moment. It leaves no disinfectant behind, and it cannot reach anything upstream. That limits it in three situations the site covers in detail:

- **A positive coliform test.** The first steps are a wellhead inspection and a shock chlorination, then a retest. A softener needs protecting during the shock, as set out in [how to protect a softener during well shock chlorination](/blog/shock-chlorination-and-water-softener/). A UV installed afterwards is a safeguard, not the repair.
- **Iron bacteria.** They live in slime on the casing, pump and pipes, upstream of any UV, and the iron they carry fouls the sleeve. The sequence that clears them is in [why iron bacteria requires more than a softener](/blog/iron-bacteria-in-well-water/).
- **Power cuts and outages.** An unpowered UV passes untreated water. VIQUA's manual says the plumbing after the UV should be disinfected whenever the system has been off. Treat that like a boil-water event, using the flushing order in [what to do with a softener during a boil-water notice](/blog/boil-water-notice-and-your-water-softener/).

![Laboratory technician in gloves examining labelled water sample tubes, the follow-up bacteria test that confirms a well UV system is working](https://images.pexels.com/photos/3735747/pexels-photo-3735747.jpeg)

Test again after installation. A bacteria test a few weeks after start-up, and once a year after that, is the only way to know the system is disinfecting.

## Who Should Buy None of These

- **Wells whose pretreatment is not in place.** If iron, hardness or turbidity exceed the limits above, buy those stages first.
- **Wells with repeat positive bacteria results after shocking.** That points to a defect in the well. Repair it, and use a Class A certified system if treatment is still needed.
- **Homes with peak flow above 14 gpm.** Choose a larger certified chamber, or split the supply, rather than running a VH410 at its 30 mJ/cm² rating.
- **Homes with frequent long power cuts and no backup.** Without power, UV does nothing, and every restart calls for disinfecting the plumbing. Chlorination may suit better.
- **Wells with high tannins.** Above 0.1 ppm they block UV, and they need their own stage ahead of it.

Choose the best UV system for well water by its flow at 40 mJ/cm² and by the pretreatment your test calls for, then install it last. For most homes that is a VIQUA VH410M behind an iron filter, a softener and a 5 micron cartridge, with a new lamp every year.
