This **APEC ROES-50 review** comes down to one fit. The ROES-50 is a plain five-stage tank RO system with no mineral stage. APEC's manual says it is certified by WQA to NSF/ANSI 58 for TDS reduction, and it costs about $231. In a home that already softens, that simplicity is an advantage. The membrane removes most of the sodium the softener added, and softened feed is what APEC's own manual recommends for long membrane life. The trade-offs are real: the "4 gallon" tank holds 2.5 to 3.1 gallons at typical pressures, and under NSF test conditions it sends about 7.5 gallons to drain for every gallon you drink.

## Why a Plain Five-Stage RO Suits a Home That Already Softens

Many newer RO systems add a sixth "mineral" or "alkaline" stage that puts calcium and magnesium back after the membrane. On the ROES-50 there is none. For a household with a water softener, three things make the plain design a good match.

**1. The membrane deals with the softener's sodium.** A softener adds about 7.9 mg/L of sodium for every grain of hardness it removes. APEC's manual says a membrane in good condition should leave product water at about 3 to 10 percent of the tap water's TDS. On 15 gpg water, that takes the sodium the softener added from about 119 mg/L to about 12 mg/L. The detail behind the sodium figure is in [sodium in softened water](/blog/sodium-in-softened-water/).

![Worked example for 15 gpg water: tap water at 15 gpg, softened water at about zero gpg with 119 mg/L of added sodium, APEC ROES-50 glass at about 12 mg/L](/diagrams/apec-roes-50-softened-feed-sodium-and-hardness.svg "The membrane takes out what the softener added. A mineral stage is about taste, not safety")

**2. Softened feed is easier on the membrane.** In its list of the four common causes of premature membrane failure, APEC's manual includes water that is "extremely hard", which "will clog up the membrane with heavy calcification. Adding a water softener will help greatly." Softened feed removes that cause. What is and is not published about membrane life on hard and softened water is collected in [reverse osmosis and water softener together](/blog/reverse-osmosis-and-water-softener-together/).

**3. One less cartridge, one less decision.** A mineral stage is a taste and pH choice. It adds a filter to replace and puts some hardness back in the glass. If you like the taste of plain RO water, the ROES-50 skips both. If you would rather have the minerals, APEC's ROES-PH75 is the same family with a calcium stage added.

The usual alternative in softened homes is to leave the kitchen cold tap on hard water. That avoids the sodium, but the drinking tap then misses every other treatment in the house. Why RO under the sink usually beats a bypass tap is covered in [should a kitchen tap bypass the softener](/blog/should-a-kitchen-tap-bypass-the-softener/).

**Plumbing note:** feed the ROES-50 from a softened cold line. If an installer has already left the kitchen cold line on hard water, either run a softened feed to the RO or accept that the membrane is working on hard water.

## What the 4 Gallon Tank Means for a Family's Day

The ROES-50 makes water slowly and stores it. So the tank, not the 50 GPD rating, decides how it feels to use.

APEC's manual gives two numbers that most product pages leave out. First, "4 gallon" is the tank's total volume, air space included. How full it gets depends on your inlet pressure:

![Four RO tanks showing the APEC ROES-50 tank holds 1.9 gallons at 40 psi, 2.5 at 50 psi, 2.8 at 60 psi and 3.1 at 70 psi or more](/diagrams/apec-roes-50-tank-fill-by-water-pressure.svg "At typical house pressure the 4 gallon tank holds about 2.5 to 2.8 gallons")

Second, delivery pressure falls as the tank empties. The manual lists about 50 psi at 3.0 gallons, 24 psi at 2.0 gallons, 14 psi at 1.0 gallon and 5 psi when empty. The last gallon comes out noticeably slower than the first.

**A family of four, worked through.** Assume about 3 gallons a day of drinking and cooking water: four people drinking about half a gallon each, plus a gallon for cooking and coffee.

- **At 60 psi** (a common city pressure), a full tank holds 2.8 gallons. That covers most of the day. The rest comes from the membrane in real time, at a trickle, unless the tank has had a 2 to 3 hour break to refill.
- **At 50 psi**, the tank holds 2.5 gallons and APEC rates production at about 30 GPD at 77°F. Use spread across the day still works. Filling a stockpot and several bottles in a row will empty it.
- **At 40 psi**, the bottom of the warranty range and the low point of many well pressure switches, the tank holds 1.9 gallons. Expect it to run low most evenings. APEC suggests a permeate pump for low-pressure homes, which increases fill and output pressure.

The fix for a big household is not a different RO system. It is a bigger tank, which is why APEC sells the ROES-50 with a 14 gallon tank as a bundle. A larger tank stores more, but it still fills at the same slow rate, so it helps households that use a lot of water at once, not ones that use a lot every hour.

If you plan to feed an ice maker as well, the manual says you need at least 50 psi into the ROES-50 for the 20 to 30 psi most ice makers want.

## 50 GPD on the Box, and What the NSF Data Says

APEC rates the ROES-50 at 50 GPD at 60 psi, and gives 30 GPD at 50 psi and 77°F. The performance data sheet in the manual, from NSF/ANSI 58 testing, shows:

- **TDS challenge:** 750 mg/L in, meeting the 75% minimum reduction
- **Efficiency rating: 11.8%**, the share of water entering the system that ends up as usable RO water with the tank in use
- **Recovery rating: 25.4%**, the same measure at the membrane with the tank bypassed
- **Production rate:** listed as 45.3 "L/gpd". The unit is unclear. If it means litres per day, it is about 12 gallons a day under the test conditions.

The efficiency rating is the number to notice. At 11.8%, about 7.5 gallons go to drain for each gallon you drink. EPA's [WaterSense page on point-of-use RO](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/point-use-reverse-osmosis-systems) says a typical system sends five gallons or more, and WaterSense-labeled systems must stay at 2.3 or less. The ROES-50 is a conventional, non-pumped tank system, and its waste figure shows it.

At 3 gallons a day, that is about 22 gallons a day to drain, roughly 8,200 gallons a year. On a city water bill the cost depends on your local rate, and is usually modest. On a softened supply, the reject water has already been softened, so it carries a small salt cost too. On a low-yield well or metered water, it is worth weighing against a tankless system with a published 2:1 or 3:1 ratio.

**Yearly cost, worked out:**

- **Softened city water, careful use:** pre-filters every 12 months, membrane and stage 5 every 4 years. About **$45 a year**.
- **Unsoftened or well water:** pre-filters every 6 months, membrane and stage 5 every 2 years. About **$87 a year**.

Over five years, including the purchase price, that is roughly **$450 to $670**. The difference between the two ends is mostly your water. Softened, chlorinated city water with prefilters changed on time is the cheapest way to run it.

**Test TDS once or twice a year,** as the manual advises. Product water at 3 to 10 percent of tap water TDS is normal. At 15 to 20 percent, the manual says it is time to change the membrane. A softener does not lower TDS, so measure the softened water feeding the RO, not the raw supply.

## Installation Notes

- **No power needed.** The ROES-50 runs on line pressure alone.
- **Cold water only.** The manual is explicit.
- **The drain line has the flow restrictor inside it.** The manual says not to remove the black drain tubing. A blocked or restricted drain is one of the four causes of early membrane failure it lists.
- **Drill a 1/4-inch hole for the drain saddle** and a 1/2-inch hole for a standard faucet, per the manual's tool list.
- **First tank:** let it fill for 2 to 3 hours, then drain it completely. Do not drink the first tank.
- **Housing O-rings:** replace them yearly or with the pre-filters. APEC recommends replacing the stage 1 to 3 housings every 5 years.

## Pros and Cons

**Pros**

- About $231, with stage 1 to 3 pre-filters at $29.95 a set
- Certified by WQA to NSF/ANSI 58 for TDS reduction, per the manual
- No mineral stage, which suits softened homes and keeps maintenance simple
- No electricity, standard 10-inch filters, a 2,000 ppm feed TDS limit
- Clear manual, with tank fill, delivery pressure and troubleshooting data

**Cons**

- 11.8% efficiency rating: about 7.5 gallons to drain per gallon delivered under test conditions
- The "4 gallon" tank holds 1.9 to 3.1 gallons depending on pressure
- Needs 40 psi or more, and 50 psi or more for a good tank fill or an ice maker
- Only a 1-year warranty
- Certified for TDS only, not the wider list of claims some competitors carry

## Who Should Not Buy the APEC ROES-50

- **Homes on metered water or a low-yield well** where 8,000 gallons a year to drain matters.
- **Low-pressure homes** under about 45 to 50 psi, unless you add a permeate pump.
- **Households that draw several gallons at once** and will not add the larger tank.
- **Anyone who needs certified reduction of lead, fluoride or other specific contaminants.** The manual's certification statement covers TDS. Check the WQA listing, or pick a system certified for the claim you need.
- **People who prefer mineral-tasting water.** Choose a model with a mineral stage.
- **Anyone still deciding about whole-house treatment.** An RO system treats one tap. Start with [salt-based vs salt-free water treatment](/blog/salt-based-vs-salt-free-water-treatment/).

## APEC ROES-50 Review: The Verdict

The APEC ROES-50 is a simple, well-documented RO system, and its simplicity is the reason to buy it if you already soften. The membrane removes the sodium your softener adds, softened feed is what APEC itself recommends for membrane life, and there is no mineral cartridge to maintain. Go in with the real numbers: about 2.5 to 2.8 gallons in the tank at normal pressure, a 2 to 3 hour refill, and a waste ratio worse than the industry's typical figure. For a two- to four-person softened home on city water, that is a fair trade for a reliable system that costs about $45 a year to run.
