**Well water treatment system cost** depends almost entirely on what your well test shows. A hard-only well needs a sediment filter and a softener, about $1,000 in equipment and roughly $1,700 installed. A well with bacteria, iron and sulfur needs an iron-sulfur filter, a softener and a UV system, about $3,180 in equipment and nearer $5,000 installed. Running costs climb in the same way, from about $155 a year to about $475. Below, each of four common profiles is broken into equipment, installation and annual running cost.

These are starting budgets, not quotes. The EPA puts it plainly: "Private well owners are responsible for delivering safe drinking water to their households," and [its private well guidance](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) starts with testing. A certified lab panel is what turns one of these profiles into your actual bill of equipment.

## The Four Profiles Over Ten Years

Upfront price favours the simple systems, and running cost widens the gap over time. Over ten years, the most complex profile costs about three times the simplest:

![Stacked bar chart of well water treatment system cost over 10 years by profile: hard only $3,250, hard plus iron $5,150, acidic plus iron $8,080, bacteria plus iron plus sulfur $9,680](/diagrams/well-water-treatment-system-cost-by-profile-10-year.svg "Every stage you add brings its own yearly cost, not just its purchase price")

The order in which these stages are plumbed matters as much as the price, because each one protects the next. The full sequence, from pressure tank to UV, is set out in [well water treatment train order](/blog/well-water-treatment-train-order/). The profiles below follow it.

**Running cost, about $370 a year.** This profile costs the most to run per stage, because calcite dissolves as it neutralizes. The model allows one to two 0.5 cubic foot bags of calcite a year, about $165. The dissolved calcite adds hardness, so the softener works harder, at about $113 of salt. Two cost traps. First, the 1.5 cubic foot neutralizers are rated at about 3.2 gpm of service flow, aimed at one to two bathrooms; a larger home needs the 2 cubic foot size, which costs more, about $830 to $1,120. Second, below pH 6.0, AFW blends calcite with Corosex, a faster media that raises pH more strongly and is used up faster. Retest the pH after the neutralizer before setting the softener.

UV needs the water in front of it to be clean. One UV maker's manual quoted in the treatment-order guide asks for iron below 0.3 ppm, hardness below 7 gpg and manganese below 0.05 ppm. That is why the iron filter and softener sit ahead of it, and why this profile cannot skip them to save money. A well with a positive bacteria test is normally shock-chlorinated and retested before any equipment is chosen. That is a one-off cost, not shown in the chart.

## What Changes the Installation Price

The installation ranges above are wide on purpose. These are the lines that move them:

| Factor | Pushes cost up when | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Number of tanks | Each stage is its own tank with a bypass | Each extra tank adds plumbing time and floor space |
| Drain | Every backwashing tank needs a drain with an air gap | A long run, or a pump to lift to the drain, adds the most |
| Electrical | UV and metered valves need an always-on outlet | An electrician may be needed if none is near |
| Floor space | The pressure tank, filters and softener must fit in sequence | Relocating equipment or adding a pad adds cost |
| Permits | Some towns require a plumbing permit | From nothing to a few hundred dollars |

Installing yourself saves most of the labor line, but every tank in a well system is heavy, every drain needs an air gap, and the stage order is easy to get wrong. A mistake there costs more than the labor saved.

## Ways to Lower the Bill Without Cutting a Stage

- **Test first, and test the right things.** Buying for a profile you do not have is the most expensive mistake. A full lab panel costs far less than one unnecessary tank.
- **Retest pH after aeration or air injection.** Removing carbon dioxide can raise pH, which may make a neutralizer smaller or unnecessary.
- **Size for flow, not for the largest model.** Oversized tanks cost more and may not backwash properly on a modest well pump.
- **Price the running cost before choosing between two systems.** A cheaper neutralizer that needs more top-ups, or a cartridge iron filter that needs a new set every year, can cost more within three to five years.

## Who Should Not Budget From These Profiles

- **Wells with arsenic, nitrate, uranium, radon, PFAS or high sodium.** These need specific treatment, often reverse osmosis at the kitchen tap or aeration, and are outside these four profiles.
- **Very high iron or manganese**, well above the iron filter's rating. A larger filter or a chemical feed system is needed, with its own running cost.
- **Low-yield wells.** Backwashing filters can draw more water than the well delivers at once. Check backwash flow against your pump test before buying.
- **Anyone without a recent lab test.** Until you have one, any profile on this page is a guess.

Once the test is back, match it to the closest profile, price each stage against the tables above, and add the yearly running cost to the comparison. The cheapest system is the one that treats only what the test found, in the right order.
