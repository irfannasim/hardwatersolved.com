The **best whole-house well water systems** are not single products. They are short sequences of equipment chosen from your water test. Four profiles cover most private wells. Hard water alone needs a spin-down filter and a softener, about $1,000 in equipment. Hard water with up to 3 ppm of dissolved iron needs a cartridge iron filter ahead of a fine-mesh softener, about $1,300. Acidic, iron-bearing water needs a neutralizer, an iron filter and a softener, about $2,260. A well with bacteria, iron and sulfur needs chlorination, filtration, a softener and UV, about $5,440. The order matters as much as the parts.

## Start From the Test, Not the Box

![Gloved hand pipetting a water sample into test tubes in a laboratory, the lab analysis a well owner needs before choosing whole-house treatment](https://images.pexels.com/photos/9259941/pexels-photo-9259941.jpeg)

Every stage in these builds is there because a number on the lab report put it there. [EPA advises](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) testing a private well every year for total coliform bacteria, nitrate, total dissolved solids and pH. That baseline is not enough to choose equipment. You also need hardness, total and dissolved iron, manganese, hydrogen sulfide, alkalinity and turbidity. Which panel to order, and how to read it, is covered in [the water test panel to order before treatment](/blog/water-test-panel-before-treatment/).

Three readings sort a well into one of the four profiles below:

- **pH under about 6.8:** profile 3. Many iron media need near-neutral water, and acidic water attacks copper plumbing whatever else is in it.
- **Coliform bacteria or a sulfur smell:** profile 4. No filter on this page disinfects except the UV stage, and UV cannot work on water that still carries iron.
- **Dissolved iron over 0.3 ppm:** profile 2, or 3 or 4 if one of the readings above also applies.

If none of those apply and hardness is the only issue, you are in profile 1.

One caution before buying anything. Well water is not fixed. A dry summer, a new well nearby or a failing casing can change iron and bacteria results from one season to the next, as explained in [why well water chemistry changes](/blog/why-well-water-chemistry-changes/). Buy for the worst result you have seen, not the best, and retest after the system goes in.

This is the build to resist adding to. A hard well with clean chemistry needs nothing more, and every extra tank adds media, maintenance and another backwash for the pump to supply.

**Why a spin-down, not a cartridge:** a 50 µm screen catches well sand and grit, and the automatic flush clears it to drain without anyone opening a housing. A 5 µm cartridge here would load up quickly on a sandy well and could choke flow to the softener.

**Running costs:** softener salt, and the occasional screen clean. 10% crosslink resin is a reasonable choice on an unchlorinated well and does not need to be fine mesh when there is no iron.

**Wrong for:** any well with measurable dissolved iron. Standard resin on iron fouls over time, so move to profile 2.

**Build:** pressure tank, cartridge iron filter, fine-mesh softener.

## Profile 3: Acidic Water With Iron

**Typical test:** hardness 8 gpg, pH 6.3, dissolved iron 3 ppm.
**Build:** pressure tank, calcite neutralizer, air-injection iron filter, softener, with a sample port between the neutralizer and the softener.

The pH reading changes everything here. The cartridge filters in profile 2 do not state a pH floor in the material we could read, and SpringWell's combination lists 6.5 as its lowest pH, above this well's 6.3. So the neutralizer goes first and the iron filter after it.

**Size the softener after the neutralizer, not from the well test.** A calcite neutralizer raises pH by dissolving calcium carbonate, which adds hardness. The 8 gpg on the report will be higher at the softener. Program it after measuring at the sample port once the neutralizer has run in. How a softener copes with the rest of a well's chemistry is covered in [water softener for well water](/blog/water-softener-for-well-water/).

**Running costs:** calcite is used up as it works and has to be topped up. AFWFilters sells 0.5 cu ft of calcite media for $110, and its upflow neutralizer page says the media needs replenishing "at least once a year on average". Add softener salt.

**Watch for:** backwash flow. Three backwashing tanks on one well means three draws on the pump. They can be timed for different nights, but each must get its full backwash flow when it runs.

**Wrong for:** pH below about 5.3. Calcite blends stop being enough there, and a soda ash feed takes over.

## Profile 4: Bacteria, Iron and Sulfur

**Typical test:** hardness 12 gpg, iron 4 ppm, hydrogen sulfide present, coliform bacteria detected.
**Build:** chlorine feed (ahead of the pressure tank), mixing tank, iron filter, carbon filter, softener, 5 µm filter, UV.

This is the profile where shortcuts fail. Chlorine oxidises the iron and sulfide and disinfects. The mixing tank gives it time to react. The iron filter catches what the chlorine turned into particles. Carbon then strips the leftover chlorine before it reaches the resin. The softener protects the UV sleeve, and UV is the last line of defence.

**Check the backwash first.** The package needs 6.5 gpm of backwash flow. Measure what your pump sustains before ordering. A well that cannot hold that flow needs smaller tanks, or storage.

**UV last, and only on prepared water.** The [VIQUA owner's manual](https://viqua.com/wp-content/uploads/MNL520585_RevB.pdf) asks for iron under 0.3 ppm, hardness under 7 gpg, manganese under 0.05 ppm, turbidity under 1 NTU and UV transmittance over 75% at the lamp. It also requires a 5 µm sediment filter ahead of the chamber. Everything before the UV in this build exists to meet those limits. VIQUA rates the lamp for about 9,000 hours and tells owners to replace it every year. Fresh Water Systems lists the S410RL-HO lamp from $160. The standard VH410 is not on NSF's Standard 55 listing; the certified versions carry a "-V" suffix and are Class B.

**A UV lamp does not fix the well.** A positive coliform result also calls for a wellhead inspection and shock chlorination. Bacteria usually get in through a defect, and the treatment train manages the water rather than closing the gap.

**The cheaper starter, and why it rarely fits this profile.** iSpring sells its WGB32BM cartridge filter with its UVF55FS 12 gpm UV as one bundle (B09BJSMZGL). We could not confirm its price, so it is not ranked. It suits a well with light bacteria, iron under 3 ppm and no sulfur smell. Even then, water harder than 7 gpg still needs a softener before the UV, which the bundle does not include.

## Who Should Buy None of These

- **Anyone without a recent full test.** Buying a profile 2 build for a well that is actually acidic leaves the plumbing corroding behind a new iron filter.
- **Wells with tannins or high nitrate.** Neither is handled by any build here. They need anion resin, or a point-of-use reverse osmosis unit for drinking water.
- **Wells pumping visible sand.** Fix the well, or add a coarse separator ahead of the pressure tank, before buying media tanks that sand will pack.
- **Low-yield wells.** If the pump cannot sustain the backwash flow of the largest tank, every build except profile 1 needs storage and a booster pump designed in first.
- **Wells that test positive for bacteria more than once after shocking.** That points to a well defect, and the money belongs in repairing the well before any treatment train.

The best whole-house well water system is the shortest sequence that meets every limit on your report, in the order each stage needs. Read your profile from the test, price the build above, and add a stage only when a number calls for it.
