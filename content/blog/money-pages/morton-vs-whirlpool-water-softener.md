**Morton vs Whirlpool water softener is a closer contest than the two brand names suggest: the same company builds both, and their nearest models use almost exactly the same salt.** The Morton M34 is rated at 5,060 grains of hardness per pound of salt and the Whirlpool WHES33 at 5,094. For a family of four on 15 gpg water, that is about 303 lb against 301 lb of salt a year. The real difference is regeneration water. At the efficient setting the Whirlpool uses about 600 fewer gallons a year, which is worth about $80 over ten years. Your salt setting changes the 10-year running cost by $410 to $500. The badge changes it by less than $80.

## Morton vs Whirlpool: One Maker, Two Badges

Neither Morton nor Whirlpool builds its own softeners. NSF's [NSF/ANSI 44 listing for Morton](https://info.nsf.org/Certified/DWTU/Listings.asp?TradeName=Morton&Standard=044) and its listing for Whirlpool both name **Water Channel Partners** of Eagan, Minnesota. Whirlpool's manual calls the company its manufacturer and warrantor. Morton's says the unit is "manufactured for" Morton.

So this is not a contest between two engineering teams. It is two badges on one platform, with different resin volumes, flow restrictors and warranty terms. Both use demand regeneration that learns your water use. Morton calls its version Look-Ahead and Whirlpool calls its version 6th Sense. Both have a salt-efficiency switch that ships turned off. That is why the comparison comes down to arithmetic, not features.

## The Closest Twins, Spec for Spec

Both manuals publish capacity at three salt doses, plus regeneration water at the lowest and highest doses. That is everything a running-cost comparison needs.

| Capacity at lowest dose | 13,700 grains @ 2.7 lb | 13,400 grains @ 2.6 lb |
| Capacity at middle dose | 29,200 grains @ 8.6 lb | 28,500 grains @ 8.3 lb |
| Capacity at highest dose | 34,900 grains @ 14.5 lb | 34,100 grains @ 14.1 lb |
| Rated efficiency (lowest dose) | 5,060 grains per lb | 5,094 grains per lb |
| Regeneration water, lowest dose | 2.3 gal per 1,000 grains | 1.9 gal per 1,000 grains |
| Regeneration water, highest dose | 31.6 gal | 33.3 gal |
| Rated service flow (manual) | 7.8 gpm | 7.2 gpm |
| Pressure drop at that flow | 14.7 psi | 14.9 psi |
| Maximum hardness / clear-water iron | 110 gpg / 9 ppm | 105 gpg / 9 ppm |

*From the manuals cited above. Whirlpool's WHES33 product page gives slightly different figures, 4,950 grains per pound and 8.0 gpm, and the 8.0 gpm figure matches NSF's listing.*

Three things stand out. The capacity curves almost overlap. The "34" and "33" in the names are capacity at the heaviest salt dose, the least efficient way to run either unit, a point covered in [advertised vs usable softener capacity](/blog/advertised-vs-usable-softener-capacity/). And the one real gap is the regeneration water at the efficient setting.

## Annual Salt Use at the Same Hardness

The salt formula is the one from the site's guide to [how much salt a water softener uses](/blog/how-much-salt-does-a-water-softener-use/): grains removed a year divided by grains per pound. For a family of four using 280 gallons a day, the house needs 1,533,000 grains removed a year at 15 gpg.

Pounds of salt a year, family of four:

| Hardness | Morton M34, lowest dose | Whirlpool WHES33, lowest dose | Either, salt-efficiency floor (4,000 gr/lb) | Morton M34, highest dose | Whirlpool WHES33, highest dose |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 gpg | 202 | 201 | 256 | 425 | 423 |
| 15 gpg | 303 | 301 | 383 | 637 | 634 |
| 20 gpg | 404 | 401 | 511 | 849 | 845 |
| 25 gpg | 505 | 502 | 639 | 1,062 | 1,056 |

*Calculated from the manuals' rated figures, not measured. Two people use about half these amounts.*

At any one setting the two brands are within six pounds a year, less than one-sixth of a bag. Move either softener from its lowest dose to its highest, though, and salt use doubles. Why a small, frequent salt dose recovers more grains per pound is explained in [what water softener salt efficiency means](/blog/water-softener-salt-efficiency/).

The table's lab figures are a best case. Both manuals say operational efficiency is "typically less" than the rated figure, because of reserve capacity, partial regenerations and other contaminants. Expect to pour in more than the table shows, but expect the gap between the two brands to stay small.

## Regeneration Water: The One Real Difference

Salt is only half the running cost. Every regeneration also sends water down the drain: backwash, brine draw, rinse and refill. The manuals give it two ways. At the lowest dose it is gallons per 1,000 grains of capacity. At the highest dose it is total gallons per regeneration.

| Family of four, 15 gpg | Morton M34 | Whirlpool WHES33 |
|---|---|---|
| Regenerations a year, lowest dose | about 112 | about 114 |
| Water per regeneration, lowest dose | about 31.5 gal | about 25.5 gal |
| Regeneration water a year, lowest dose | about 3,530 gal | about 2,910 gal |
| Regenerations a year, highest dose | about 44 | about 45 |
| Regeneration water a year, highest dose | about 1,390 gal | about 1,500 gal |

At the efficient setting both softeners regenerate about every three days. The Whirlpool uses about six fewer gallons each time, which adds up to about 600 gallons a year. At the highest dose the order flips, but the gap is only about 110 gallons. Either way it is a small share of a household that uses about 100,000 gallons a year. How regeneration water is counted, and where it goes, is covered in [how much water regeneration uses](/blog/how-much-water-does-regeneration-use/).

## Regeneration Control and the Efficiency Switch

Both softeners decide when to regenerate from a meter and a learned usage pattern, and both pick the salt dose for each regeneration. The difference between them is in the defaults:

- **Salt efficiency ships off on both.** Whirlpool's manual says that with the feature on, the softener will "operate at salt efficiencies of 4000 grains of hardness per pound of salt or higher," and "may recharge more often using smaller salt dosage and less water." Morton's manual makes the same 4,000 grains-per-pound promise. California installations must have it on.
- **Iron cleaning costs water.** Whirlpool's clear-water iron feature adds a short backwash and fast rinse before each regeneration. Its manual says to switch it off on water with no iron or sediment, to save water.
- **Iron sizing is identical.** Both manuals tell you to add 5 to the hardness setting for every 1 ppm of clear-water iron.

If you are coming from an old timer softener, either one will cut salt sharply. The reason is explained in [metered vs timer-based water softeners](/blog/metered-vs-timer-based-water-softeners/).

The prices are close enough that a sale decides it. The warranty is not as close. Whirlpool's first year includes labor and its electronics are covered for three years. Morton's manual covers non-tank parts for one year with no labor. If you would rather not fit a replacement circuit board yourself in year two, that tips the choice toward Whirlpool. Service life is the same question for both. Tanks outlast valves, and the rotor, seals and board wear first, as covered in [how long a water softener lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-water-softener-last/). Parts for both come from the same maker.

## Morton or Whirlpool: Which to Buy

- **Buy the Whirlpool WHES33** if you are on metered water and sewer at a high rate, if you want labor covered in year one and electronics for three years, or if Lowe's is your nearest store.
- **Buy the Morton M34** if Menards has it on sale, if you want its slightly higher rated flow of 7.8 gpm, or if you prefer a bigger resin bed by a few hundredths of a cubic foot.
- **Either way,** switch on the salt-efficiency setting, set the hardness correctly with iron added, and log the salt you actually pour in for the first three months.

## Who Should Buy Neither

- **Houses that run more than about 8 gpm at peak.** Both twins are rated at 7 to 8 gpm. Two showers and a washer at once can exceed that.
- **Wells with red-water, bacterial or high iron.** Both are rated for up to 9 ppm of clear-water iron only, and Wisconsin requires extra treatment above 5 ppm.
- **Very hard water above about 25 gpg in a big household.** At the efficient setting both would regenerate every other day or so. A larger resin bed makes more sense.
- **Anyone hoping to avoid salt altogether.** Both are salt-based ion exchange softeners. The trade-offs with conditioners are in [salt-based vs salt-free water treatment](/blog/salt-based-vs-salt-free-water-treatment/).

Morton vs Whirlpool water softener comes down to warranty, store and price on the day. The salt bill will be the same within a bag a year. Spend your attention on the efficiency setting instead, because that is where the $400 to $500 over ten years really is.
