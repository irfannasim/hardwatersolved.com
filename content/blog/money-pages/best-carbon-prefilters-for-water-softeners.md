The **best carbon prefilters** for a water softener depend on one thing first: whether your city uses free chlorine or chloramine. On **free chlorine**, a non-backwashing carbon tank such as the **Aquasana Rhino EQ-1000** is the simplest buy, rated for 1,000,000 gallons or 10 years. That is about ten years for a family of four. On **chloramine**, buy **catalytic carbon** in a backwashing tank, such as **AFWFilters' 1.5 cubic foot catalytic carbon system** on a Fleck 5600SXT, and size it for your peak flow.

Gallon ratings are chlorine figures. On chloramine the same tank protects a fraction of the flow, and this page shows how much.

## First, Find Out Which Disinfectant You Have

Your utility's annual Consumer Confidence Report names the disinfectant. You can also test at a tap before the softener with a kit that reads free and total chlorine. If total runs well above free, most of the residual is chloramine. The EPA's page on [chloramines in drinking water](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/chloramines-drinking-water) says more than one in five Americans drinks chloraminated water, so do not assume.

Whether you need carbon at all is a separate question, and the answer is often no on a low free chlorine residual. The payback is worked through in [when city chlorine requires carbon pretreatment](/blog/carbon-filter-before-water-softener/). What chloramine does to resin, and why ordinary carbon struggles with it, is in [how chloramine affects a water softener](/blog/how-chloramine-affects-a-water-softener/). This page assumes you have decided to buy and covers which one. And if your softener has simply stopped softening, rule out a brine-side fault before blaming chlorine: [why a water softener is not using salt](/blog/water-softener-not-using-salt/) covers that check.

## Chlorine vs Chloramine: Why the Same Tank Protects Less

Carbon removes free chlorine fast. It removes chloramine slowly, by breaking it down on the carbon surface. The Water Quality Association's [chloramine fact sheet](https://wqa.org/resources/chloramine/) notes that chloramine can need a carbon contact time of 10 minutes or more for complete removal. Catalytic carbon is made to speed that reaction, and the site's planning figure for it is about 3 minutes, against about 1.5 minutes for free chlorine.

Contact time is simple arithmetic: **minutes = media volume in gallons ÷ flow in gpm**, and one cubic foot of media holds 7.48 gallons. So the flow a tank protects is its media volume in gallons divided by the contact time.

![Bar chart of the flow each carbon prefilter protects: on free chlorine 5 to 7.5 gpm, on chloramine about 0.75 to 1.1 gpm for standard carbon and 3.7 gpm for 1.5 cu ft catalytic carbon](/diagrams/carbon-prefilter-softener-flow-protected-chlorine-vs-chloramine.svg "On chloramine, only catalytic carbon gets near household flow")

Three things follow:

- **On free chlorine, all four protect normal flows.** The 1.5 cubic foot tanks cover about 7.5 gpm, close to a typical 8 gpm household peak.
- **On chloramine, standard carbon covers about 1 gpm.** That is a single tap. At a shower or two, most of the chloramine reaches the resin.
- **Catalytic carbon gets you to about 3.7 gpm** in a 1.5 cubic foot tank. AFWFilters rates this system at 3 gpm service flow. For an 8 gpm peak on chloramine, the site's sizing puts the need at roughly 3.2 cubic feet of catalytic media. AFWFilters sells 2.0, 2.5 and 3.0 cubic foot catalytic systems for that.

## Cartridge Prefilters: When They Make Sense

A large 20 inch cartridge housing is cheaper and needs no drain. It also holds far less carbon than a tank, so it gives little contact time at whole-house flows and needs frequent changes. NDSU Extension's rule of thumb for high-volume carbon filters is every three to six months of use. A cartridge suits a small home on low free chlorine, or a temporary fix. For resin protection on chloramine, it is not enough.

## Fitting a Carbon Prefilter Ahead of a Softener

1. **Order: sediment, then carbon, then softener.** Give each unit its own bypass.
2. **Schedule backwashing tanks apart.** If both units backwash, set the carbon to regenerate first and the softener after it finishes.
3. **Check the combined pressure drop.** A carbon tank and softener in series can take 15 to 25 psi at busy flow.
4. **Test yearly.** Check total chlorine between the carbon and the softener. When it appears, the media is due.

## Who Should Buy None of These

- **Low free chlorine homes.** Under about 0.5 mg/L, 10 percent crosslink resin at the next rebed is often cheaper than carbon. See the payback figures in the carbon pretreatment guide linked above.
- **Well owners with no chlorinator.** There is no chlorine to remove from the resin's point of view.
- **Chloramine homes with a peak flow far above 3 gpm, looking at the 1.5 cubic foot tanks.** Size up to 2.5 or 3.0 cubic feet of catalytic media instead.
- **Homes that rarely use water for long periods.** Carbon removes the disinfectant, and a bed that sits unused can grow bacteria. Flush it after time away.

For most free chlorine homes, the Rhino is the simplest prefilter, with no media change for about ten years. On chloramine, buy catalytic carbon sized for your peak flow. Measure the disinfectant before you buy, because the same tank does a very different job on each.
