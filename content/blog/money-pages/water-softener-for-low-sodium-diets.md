The best **water softener for low-sodium diets** is an ordinary, well-sized salt softener with a reverse osmosis (RO) unit at the kitchen tap. There is no such thing as a low-sodium softener. Every salt-regenerated softener adds about 7.9 mg of sodium per litre for each grain per gallon (gpg) of hardness it removes, whatever the brand. What you can choose is where that sodium ends up. RO at the drinking tap removes most of it for the cost of the unit and its replacement filters. Switching the softener to potassium chloride removes it at every tap, but adds the much higher cost of potassium bags to every regeneration for as long as the softener runs. A hard bypass line adds none, but delivers hard, untreated water.

The sodium limit itself is a medical question. Ask the clinician who manages the diet for the number, then use the figures below to pick the setup that meets it.

## Sodium in a Glass at Your Hardness

Ion exchange swaps each calcium or magnesium ion for two sodium ions, so the sodium added depends only on how much hardness the softener removes. The full derivation is in [sodium in softened water](/blog/sodium-in-softened-water/). For an 8 ounce glass it comes to about 1.9 mg per gpg.

| Hardness removed | Softened, 8 oz glass | After RO (3 to 10% passes) | Potassium chloride instead | Hard bypass tap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 gpg | about 19 mg sodium | about 1 to 2 mg | ~0 mg sodium, about 32 mg potassium | 0 mg added |
| 15 gpg | about 28 mg | about 1 to 3 mg | ~0 mg sodium, about 47 mg potassium | 0 mg added |
| 20 gpg | about 37 mg | about 1 to 4 mg | ~0 mg sodium, about 63 mg potassium | 0 mg added |
| 25 gpg | about 47 mg | about 1 to 5 mg | ~0 mg sodium, about 79 mg potassium | 0 mg added |
| 30 gpg | about 56 mg | about 2 to 6 mg | ~0 mg sodium, about 95 mg potassium | 0 mg added |

*Sodium added only. The RO column uses the 3 to 10 percent product-water range in the APEC ROES-50 manual, applied to the added sodium. Potassium is about 13.4 mg/L per gpg removed.*

Two corrections to your own number:

1. **Add the raw-water sodium.** Many supplies already carry some. EPA's advisory found a median of 16.4 mg/L across a national survey of groundwater systems. Your utility report lists it, or a well test will. RO removes this too. A bypass tap and potassium chloride do not.
2. **Multiply by what you actually drink and cook with.** Cooking water, coffee and tea count. Showering and laundry do not.

For scale, EPA's [drinking water advisory on sodium](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2014-09/documents/support_cc1_sodium_dwreport.pdf) set its 20 mg/L guidance level "for those individuals restricted to a total sodium intake of 500 mg/day". Water softened from 15 gpg carries about 118 mg/L of added sodium. Whether that matters for a particular diet is for the clinician to say. Whether it is generally safe to drink is covered in [is softened water safe to drink](/blog/is-softened-water-safe-to-drink/).

## Setup 1: RO at the Kitchen Tap, the Pick for Most Homes

An RO membrane rejects dissolved salts, including the sodium the softener added and the sodium the water carried in. It also protects itself on softened feed: hard water scales membranes. That makes RO and a softener a natural pair, as [reverse osmosis and a water softener together](/blog/reverse-osmosis-and-water-softener-together/) explains. The four picks below differ on flow, filter cost and certification.

## Setup 2: Potassium Chloride Instead of Salt

Switching the brine tank to potassium chloride makes the softener exchange potassium for hardness instead of sodium. The softened water then carries almost no added sodium at any tap, including bathroom taps, the fridge line and a second kitchen.

The cost is the drawback. A typical household using 480 lb of salt a year needs about 20 percent more potassium chloride to remove the same hardness, about 576 lb. A bag of potassium chloride costs several times as much as a bag of salt, so the yearly regenerant bill rises by a large multiple, every year the softener runs. Bulk pricing narrows the gap a little, and a softer supply or smaller household shrinks it. The full trade-off, including the setting change your valve needs, is in [potassium chloride vs sodium chloride in a softener](/blog/potassium-chloride-vs-sodium-chloride-softener/).

There is a medical caution too. Potassium-softened water adds about 47 mg of potassium to a glass at 15 gpg. Most people handle that easily. People with kidney disease or on some blood pressure medicines may be told to limit potassium, and for them this setup swaps one restricted mineral for another. Ask before switching.

Potassium chloride also leaves the raw water's own sodium in place. If the supply already carries a lot, this setup will not bring the drinking water down to a low figure.

## Setup 3: A Hard Bypass Line to the Kitchen

The oldest answer is to run the kitchen cold tap from a pipe teed off before the softener. It adds no sodium, needs no filters and costs nothing to run. A plumber prices it as a one-off job. It is cheap at rough-in and dearer when the pipe has to reach a finished kitchen.

It has two weaknesses. The tap delivers hard water, so the kettle and coffee maker scale up again. And if the tee is placed at the main, the drinking tap skips every other filter in the house: iron, sediment and carbon as well as the softener. If you have those, the tee has to go after the filters and just before the softener. Where to tee it, and why RO usually wins, is covered in [should a kitchen tap bypass the softener](/blog/should-a-kitchen-tap-bypass-the-softener/).

A bypass line also leaves the raw water's own sodium in the glass. On a well near the coast or one affected by road salt, that can be the larger number.

## Which Setup Fits Your House

![Decision chart for a water softener for low-sodium diets: limit potassium too points away from potassium chloride, a treated or well supply points to RO, an easy cold line points to a hard bypass line](/diagrams/low-sodium-diet-softener-setup-decision-chart.svg "Most households end at RO; potassium suits those who want low sodium at every tap")

- **Most softened homes:** RO at the kitchen tap. Start with the APEC ROES-50 on cost, or the Waterdrop G3P600 for flow and the TDS readout.
- **Plain RO water goes undrunk:** the Home Master TMAFC, accepting the lack of a system listing.
- **Low sodium wanted at every tap,** and nobody limiting potassium: potassium chloride, with the running cost above.
- **New build, city water, no other treatment:** a hard line fitted at rough-in is the cheapest option of all.

## Who Should Buy None of These

- **Anyone who has not yet been given a sodium limit.** Get the number first. For many diets, the sodium in softened water is small next to food, and no equipment is needed.
- **Households with high raw-water sodium and a bypass plan.** A hard tap carries the raw sodium straight to the glass. Test the supply, and use RO if it is high.
- **Renters who cannot drill a sink.** None of the under-sink units suits you. A countertop RO unit needs no plumbing.
- **Homes without a softener at all.** If the water is not softened, no sodium is being added. Decide whether you need a softener first, then plan the drinking tap.

The softener brand does not change the sodium. The hardness it removes does. Size the softener for the water, put RO at the tap people drink from, and take the glass figures above to the person managing the diet.
