The **best iron filters for well water** are picked by technology first and brand second. For most wells with up to about 10 ppm of iron and little manganese or sulfur, the AFWFilters Air Injection Silver 10 is the simplest choice. Heavy iron with a rotten-egg smell needs a bigger air injection tank such as Oceanic's 12x52. High manganese points to greensand, acidic water below pH 6.8 points to Katalox Light, and iron bacteria rule out every filter until the well is disinfected.

Four lab results decide which of those you are: iron plus manganese, pH, hydrogen sulfide and any sign of iron bacteria. The chart below runs them in order, and each pick lists its oxidant, the backwash flow your well must supply and the lowest pH its maker states.

## Match the Technology to Four Test Results

![Decision chart for choosing the best iron filter for well water by iron bacteria, pH, iron plus manganese, hydrogen sulfide and manganese, with a pick for each answer](/diagrams/iron-filter-for-well-water-technology-selection-chart.svg "Five checks, in order, and the first yes decides the technology")

Read the chart from the top and stop at the first yes. The order matters, because the early checks make some technologies useless however much iron they are rated for.

1. **Iron bacteria.** Slime, red-brown gel in the toilet tank or a swampy smell means the well needs disinfecting before any filter goes in. Bacteria coat media and valve ports, and a new filter fouls in months.
2. **pH.** NDSU Extension says greensand "will probably not filter out the iron and manganese adequately" below pH 6.8. Katalox Light lists an inlet range of 5.8 to 10.5, so it is the low-pH choice. The other option is to raise the pH first.
3. **Iron plus manganese.** [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/iron-and-manganese-in-private-water-systems) recommends oxidizing filters such as greensand for combined iron and manganese of about 3 to 10 mg/L. Above 10 mg/L it recommends chlorine (or another oxidant) followed by filtration.
4. **Hydrogen sulfide.** A rotten-egg smell from the cold taps means sulfide in the well. It uses up the oxidizing capacity that would otherwise go to iron.
5. **Manganese.** It needs more oxidizing push than iron and is the first thing a light-duty air injection unit lets through.

**Iron form matters less here than it does for a softener.** All five picks oxidize and then filter, so they take both dissolved and already-rusted iron. The form still tells you something. Water that is orange at the tap has met air or chlorine somewhere, and [dissolved vs oxidized iron in well water](/blog/dissolved-vs-oxidized-iron-in-well-water/) shows how to find out where. If the iron is dissolved, under a few ppm and the water is hard, a softener alone may do. That trade-off is laid out in [water softener vs iron filter](/blog/water-softener-vs-iron-filter/).

- **Backwash flow:** 5 gpm, from AFW's air injection comparison table, which also says the 1 cu ft systems need a backwash at least every 3 days.
- **Lowest pH:** not stated by AFW.
- **Tank and warranty:** 10x54 tank, 62 inches tall overall. AFW lists 1 year on the control head and 10 years on the tank for its air injection systems.
- **Price:** $1,199 on sale, $1,399 standard, at AFWFilters. [See it on Amazon (B004FVZHLC)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004FVZHLC)

This is the default because it uses nothing but air and a backwash. There is no chemical tank and no regenerant. The media is plain filter media, and AFW sells a replacement 1 cu ft Filter Ag Plus kit for about $195. **Wrong for:** wells with a strong sulfur smell. Sulfide above about 4 ppm takes this unit past its rating.

## When Chlorine Injection Is the Right Answer

None of the five picks is the best tool for every well. Penn State's guidance for combined iron and manganese above 10 mg/L is oxidation followed by filtration, usually a small chlorine feed pump, a mixing tank or pipe coil for contact time, and a backwashing filter after it. NDSU adds that chlorine oxidizes iron fastest at about pH 8.0 and manganese at about 8.5, and it also controls iron bacteria. A carbon filter afterwards removes the chlorine taste.

It is more work: the solution tank needs refilling, and the filter and carbon still need servicing. But it has no ceiling in the way a rated media does. AFWFilters sells chlorine injection packages on its own store. We could not match one to an Amazon listing on the day we checked, so none is ranked here.

## Backwash Flow: The Spec That Rules Out More Wells Than Iron Does

Every pick on this page needs between 5 and 10 gpm of backwash flow, held for the whole cycle. That is roughly twice the 2.5 gpm Pentair lists for a softener in a 10-inch tank, because an iron filter bed has to be lifted and scrubbed hard enough to release the iron it has trapped.

Before you order, measure what your pump delivers with a bucket and a watch while nothing else in the house is running. Then compare it with the figure in the table at the top:

- **Under 5 gpm:** none of these picks is a safe fit as listed. Ask the seller about a smaller tank or a storage-tank setup.
- **5 to 7 gpm:** the 10-inch and 9-inch picks fit once your figure clears theirs, which runs from 5 gpm (Silver 10) to 5.6 gpm (KL-1054). There is little to spare.
- **10 gpm or more:** the Oceanic 12x52 becomes an option.

If you also own a softener, set the two units to regenerate at different hours. Both drawing from the pump at once is the commonest reason an iron filter is under-washed. The full equipment order is set out in [iron filter before water softener](/blog/iron-filter-before-water-softener/).

## What Running an Iron Filter Costs

The purchase price is most of the bill, because none of the air injection or catalytic picks uses a chemical. Running costs come from three things:

- **Backwash water.** Every cycle sends water to drain. Air injection units backwash often: daily for the Oceanic, at least every 3 days for AFW's Silver.
- **Media replacement.** AFW sells a replacement Filter Ag Plus kit for the Silver 10 at about $195. Oceanic says its filter media lasts about 5 years on average.
- **Chlorine, if you use it.** It applies only to a chlorine feed ahead of greensand or to a full chlorine injection system. It is a monthly supply, not a one-off.

Iron filters also save money downstream. With the iron removed, a softener after it no longer needs iron cleaner or extra salt to cope with iron, as [can a water softener remove iron](/blog/can-a-water-softener-remove-iron/) explains.

## Who Should Buy None of These

- **Wells with iron bacteria.** Disinfect first. The Minnesota Department of Health's [iron bacteria guidance](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/environment/water/wells/waterquality/ironbacteria.html) lists shock chlorination as the most common treatment and warns that it may need repeating.
- **Iron under 0.3 ppm.** Penn State's recommended limit for drinking water is 0.3 mg/L. Below it, iron rarely stains, and a filter adds cost for little visible gain.
- **Hard water with low, dissolved iron.** If the iron is clear-water iron within a fine-mesh softener's rating and the pH is above 6.7, one softener can handle both. In that case, look at well-water softeners with fine-mesh resin rather than a separate iron filter.
- **Wells that cannot hold 5 gpm.** Every pick here needs at least that for backwashing.
- **Yellow or tea-coloured water that is not iron.** Tannins need a different media, and an iron filter will not clear them.

The best iron filter for well water is the one whose technology matches your four results, with a backwash your pump can hold. For most wells that is a plain air injection unit. The chart moves you to something else only when the water gives a reason.
