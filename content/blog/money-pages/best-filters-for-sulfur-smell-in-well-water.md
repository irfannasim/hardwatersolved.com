Under 2 ppm, a backwashing catalytic carbon filter works if the water holds enough oxygen, and AFW's Silver Series air injection filter works if it does not. From 2 to 6 ppm, Oceanic's 12x52 air injection tank with catalytic carbon has the rating to spare. Above 6 ppm, chlorine injection with a contact tank is the usual answer.

Before any of that, smell the hot and cold taps separately. If only the hot water smells, the cause is almost always the water heater's anode rod, and a $1,000 filter will not touch it.

## Find the Source Before You Buy a Filter

![Woman holding a glass of water up to smell it in a sunlit room, the sniff test that separates hot-tap anode odour from sulfur coming from the well](https://images.pexels.com/photos/5760876/pexels-photo-5760876.jpeg)

![Decision chart for choosing a filter for sulfur smell in well water: hot-tap-only odour goes to the anode rod, softener-only odour to sanitizing, and well sulfide to three H2S bands with a pick for each](/diagrams/sulfur-smell-well-water-filter-decision-chart.svg "Two of the three sources need no filter at all")

Fill a glass from the cold tap, carry it away from the sink and smell it. Do the same with the hot tap. Then do it once more at a tap ahead of any softener, such as an outside spigot. The pattern tells you where the sulfide comes from:

- **Hot only.** The water heater is making it. Penn State explains that the heater's magnesium anode rod "can chemically reduce sulfates to form hydrogen sulfide". No whole-house filter fixes that.
- **Softened taps smell, the outside tap does not.** Sulfate-reducing bacteria are growing in the softener or the house plumbing. Penn State notes softeners can occasionally provide "a favorable environment" for them. Sanitize the softener and pipes. The full test, including the first-draw clue, is in [why a sulfur smell remains after softening](/blog/sulfur-smell-after-water-softener/).
- **Every tap smells, hot and cold, including the outside tap.** The sulfide is in the well water. Now a filter is the answer, and the next step is a number.

## Test the Sulfide Before Choosing a Band

Hydrogen sulfide is a gas and leaves a sample bottle within hours. Penn State says testing "must be done in the home, or the sample must be chemically stabilized before being sent to a commercial testing lab". Ask a state-accredited lab for a preserved bottle and take the sample from a raw-water tap before any treatment.

Ask for two more results on the same visit:

- **Iron and manganese.** They are often present with sulfide, and they use the same oxidizing capacity. A filter rated for 10 ppm of sulfide alone may not manage 4 ppm of sulfide plus 5 ppm of iron.
- **Dissolved oxygen.** This decides whether catalytic carbon can work without air. Penn State says catalytic carbon "requires a minimum of 4.0 mg/L of dissolved oxygen in the source water", and deep wells often have less.

Smell alone is a poor gauge. Penn State notes most people can detect hydrogen sulfide "well below 0.5 mg/L", so a strong smell can still be a low reading.

## Seller Ratings vs What Penn State Says Each Method Handles

![Scale of hydrogen sulfide from 0 to 18 ppm comparing Penn State's normal ranges for carbon, aeration, oxidizing filters and chlorination with seller ratings of 4, 10 and 17 ppm](/diagrams/hydrogen-sulfide-ppm-treatment-guidance-vs-seller-ratings.svg "Air injection sellers rate their tanks far past the 2 mg/L Penn State gives for aeration")

Penn State's guide gives these working ranges:

| Method | Penn State's guidance |
|---|---|
| Activated carbon | May be effective under about 1.0 mg/L, with a large whole-house bed |
| Aeration | "Normally not used when hydrogen sulfide concentrations exceed about 2 mg/L" |
| Oxidizing filters (greensand type) | Can remove up to 2 to 3 mg/L, with more frequent regeneration as levels rise |
| Catalytic carbon | Handles "much higher" levels than activated carbon, but needs 4.0 mg/L of dissolved oxygen |
| Chlorination | Works at any level, "most often applied" above 6.0 mg/L, with at least 20 minutes of contact time |

The sellers' figures are much higher: 4 ppm for AFW's Silver Series air injection, 10 ppm for AFW's catalytic carbon and 17 ppm for Oceanic's 12x52. That gap does not make either side wrong. A modern air injection tank is not plain aeration. The media in the tank adsorbs and oxidizes sulfide as well. But the extension figures are the safe design points, and a seller's figure is the best case. That is why the bands on this page put each pick well below its listed ceiling.

## Over 6 ppm: When Chlorine Injection Is the Better Buy

Above about 6 ppm, Penn State's guidance is continuous oxidation. A small feed pump injects chlorine solution ahead of a mixing tank or pipe coil sized for "at least 20 minutes of contact time". The sulfide turns into solid sulfur, a backwashing filter catches it, and a carbon filter removes the leftover chlorine taste. Potassium permanganate feeding ahead of a greensand filter is another option Penn State describes for this range.

It is more maintenance than air injection. The chlorine tank needs refilling, and the filter and carbon need servicing. But there is no rating to run out of, and chlorine also tackles the sulfate-reducing bacteria that make the gas in the first place. AFWFilters sells complete chlorine injection packages with a mixing tank, iron filter and carbon on its own store. We could not match one to an Amazon listing, so none is ranked here.

Oceanic's 12x52 is rated to 17 ppm, so it can work in this band. Choose it only if your result sits well inside that, the pump can hold 10 gpm, and you accept that you are relying on a seller's best case.

## Hot Water Only: Replace the Anode, Not the Plumbing

If the cold water is clean and only the hot smells, the fix costs far less than any filter. Penn State's recommendation: "Replacement of the magnesium rod with an aluminum rod should eliminate the rotten egg odor while maintaining corrosion protection for the heater." It warns that simply removing the rod "could cause increased corrosion and reduced life of the hot water heater and will likely void the manufacturer's warranty."

Two things make the anode check worth doing even if you do buy a filter:

- **Softened water wears anodes faster.** Bradford White's manual says a softener "may increase the speed of anode consumption", as covered in [how a water softener affects a storage water heater](/blog/water-softener-and-storage-water-heater/).
- **A heater can keep smelling after the well is treated.** Sulfate that a sulfide filter does not remove can still react with a magnesium rod. If the hot water smells after a filter goes in, look at the anode before blaming the filter.

## Install It Ahead of the Softener

Whichever filter you choose, it goes before the softener and after the pressure tank. Sulfide fouls resin, and any oxidizer strong enough to destroy sulfide can also damage resin. If you use chlorine, the carbon filter sits between the chlorine and the softener. Set the filter and softener to backwash at different hours, so the pump does not have to supply both at once.

## Who Should Buy None of These

- **Hot-water-only odour.** Replace the anode rod first.
- **Smell only on softened lines.** Sanitize the softener and plumbing. A filter on the well will not reach bacteria living downstream of it.
- **Recurring sulfur bacteria in the well.** Penn State notes that shock chlorination helps, but the bacteria and smell often return quickly. If they do, continuous chlorination is the long-term answer, not a media filter.
- **A smell at one fixture only.** That points to the fixture's drain or a hose, not the water.
- **Wells that cannot hold 5 gpm of backwash.** The air injection picks need at least that and the 12x52 needs 10. Ask AFW for the catalytic carbon unit's figure before counting on it.

The best filter for sulfur smell in well water is the one rated well above your measured sulfide, fed with the oxygen it needs and a backwash your pump can hold. Find the source first, test second, and buy last.
