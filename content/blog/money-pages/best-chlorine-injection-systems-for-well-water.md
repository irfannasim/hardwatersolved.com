The **best chlorine injection systems** for well water are built from four parts rather than bought as one box: an adjustable Stenner metering pump, a contact tank sized to your peak flow, a backwashing filter for the iron the chlorine oxidizes, and a catalytic carbon tank that strips the chlorine before it reaches softener resin. For most homes that means the Stenner 45MHP10 (0.5 to 10 gallons a day), a 120-gallon fiberglass retention tank and a 1.5 cubic foot catalytic carbon tank. Chlorine is the right tool where air injection keeps failing: iron bacteria, heavy sulfur, or a well that needs continuous disinfection.

The parts matter more than the brand. A pump too small for your chlorine demand, or a contact tank too small for your flow, will not do the job, however good the badge.

## The Four Parts of a Chlorine Injection System

![Chlorine injection system for well water in plumbing order: well, metering pump injecting at the line, pressure tank, contact tank, backwashing iron filter, catalytic carbon tank, then the water softener](/diagrams/chlorine-injection-system-component-train-well-water.svg "Carbon always sits between the chlorine and the softener resin")

Every working system has the same order. Each part does one job the others cannot:

1. **Metering pump and solution tank.** The pump pushes a measured stream of diluted chlorine into the line from the well. Nebraska Extension's guide to [continuous chlorination](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1496/2016/html/view) says the feed pump should be wired to the well pump's pressure switch, "so that the chemical pump operates only when the water well pump is operating." That way the dose follows the flow.
2. **Contact tank.** Chlorine does not work instantly. The same guide notes that "the time between the pump and the nearest faucet is usually one minute or less" in a home, which is why a retention tank is added.
3. **Backwashing filter.** Chlorine turns dissolved iron, manganese and sulfide into particles. Something has to catch them. On a well with more than a trace of iron, that is a dedicated backwashing iron filter. AFWFilters' packaged trains use a Filox or air injection tank in this position.
4. **Carbon tank.** Activated carbon removes the leftover chlorine and its taste. Nebraska Extension lists this as the standard follow-on stage. For a well with a softener, it is not optional.

Skipping any stage moves the problem downstream. Without the contact tank, bacteria survive. Without the filter, rust-coloured particles reach the carbon and blind it. Without the carbon, chlorine reaches the softener.

## Sizing the Metering Pump: Dose, Flow and Bleach Strength

The pump's job is to deliver enough chlorine to satisfy the water's demand and leave a small free residual. The demand comes from what the chlorine reacts with. Standard water-treatment figures for the chlorine needed per ppm of each are:

| What chlorine reacts with | Chlorine needed (theoretical) |
|---|---|
| Ferrous iron | about 0.64 ppm per ppm of iron |
| Manganese | about 1.3 ppm per ppm of manganese |
| Hydrogen sulfide | about 2.2 ppm per ppm, oxidized to sulfur, and several times more if oxidized fully to sulfate |

These are the chemistry minimums. Organic matter and bacteria add more, so treat the result as a starting point and adjust from a residual test after the contact tank.

**Worked example.** A well with 3 ppm of iron and 3 ppm of hydrogen sulfide needs about 1.9 ppm for the iron and 6.6 ppm for the sulfide. Add an illustrative 1 ppm residual and the target dose is roughly 10 ppm.

The pump only runs while the well pump runs, so what matters is how fast it feeds compared with the well pump's flow. The required strength of the solution in the tank is:

> **Solution strength (ppm) = target dose (ppm) × well pump gpm × 1,440 ÷ pump gallons per day**

Plain household bleach is roughly 60,000 ppm (6% sodium hypochlorite). Check your label, because strengths vary. Here is what each Stenner pump needs at an 8 gpm well pump, using its full rated output:

| Target dose | 45MHP2 (3 gpd max) needs | 45MHP10 (10 gpd max) needs |
|---|---|---|
| 3 ppm (bacteria, light iron) | about 11,500 ppm: 1 part bleach to 4 parts water | about 3,500 ppm: 1 part bleach to 16 parts water |
| 10 ppm (the worked example) | about 38,400 ppm: nearly neat bleach | about 11,500 ppm: 1 part bleach to 4 parts water |
| 20 ppm (heavy sulfur) | about 76,800 ppm: stronger than household bleach, so it cannot be done | about 23,000 ppm: 1 part bleach to 1.6 parts water |

*Illustrative. Figures assume 6% bleach and the pump at 100% of its dial. Real output varies; Stenner's [specification sheet](https://store.hipco.com/images/additional/Stenner_45_85_Adj_Pump_Specs.pdf) recommends field calibration of every metering pump.*

The table is why the 45MHP10 is the default pick. At light doses it runs at a low dial setting with a weak, forgiving solution. At heavy doses it still has headroom. The 45MHP2 makes sense only for low demand: disinfection of clear water, or a slow well pump.

## Sizing the Contact Tank by Peak Flow

![Chart of nominal chlorine contact time in minutes for 40, 80 and 120 gallon contact tanks at 6, 8, 10 and 12 gpm peak flow, against a 20 to 30 minute target band](/diagrams/chlorine-contact-tank-minutes-by-peak-flow-chart.svg "Only the 120-gallon tank reaches 20 minutes, and only at 6 gpm")

Contact time is simply the tank's volume divided by the flow through it:

> **Nominal minutes = tank gallons ÷ peak flow (gpm)**

Size it on the busiest moment of the day, when two showers and a washing machine run together, not on average use. Water Filters of America, a treatment dealer, gives 20 to 30 minutes of contact for iron bacteria and coliform in its chemical feed guide. Oxidizing sulfur or iron alone at a higher dose needs less.

| Tank | 6 gpm | 8 gpm | 10 gpm | 12 gpm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 40 gal | about 7 min | 5 min | 4 min | about 3 min |
| 80 gal | about 13 min | 10 min | 8 min | about 7 min |
| 120 gal | 20 min | 15 min | 12 min | 10 min |

*Nominal. Real contact time is shorter, because some water takes a quicker path through the tank. A tank with an inlet diffuser or distributor tube does better than an open one.*

Two things follow. First, for bacteria the 120-gallon tank is the smallest that comes close at typical household flows. Second, at 10 gpm and above even 120 gallons falls short, and the usual fix is a higher chlorine residual, which raises the carbon stage's workload. Ask whoever designs your system which trade-off they are making.

## The Filter and Carbon Stages

After the contact tank, the water carries oxidized particles and leftover chlorine. Two tanks deal with them.

**The filter for oxidized iron and sulfur.** If the well has more than a trace of iron or manganese, put a dedicated backwashing filter before the carbon. The Water Systems Council calls for a backwashing filter rated at 10 microns. AFWFilters' packaged trains use a Filox tank here, with 6.5 gpm minimum backwash on its 9 gpm package. Check that your well pump can deliver that backwash flow.

**The carbon tank.** This is the softener's protection. Pick a backwashing tank on an automatic valve, so oxidized particles that get past the filter are flushed rather than packed into the bed.

### Catalytic carbon backwashing tank, Fleck 5600SXT, 1.5 cu ft

- **What it is:** 1.5 cubic feet of catalytic carbon on a Fleck 5600SXT metered valve; the listing says it removes chlorine, chloramines and hydrogen sulfide odour
- **Smaller sibling:** AFWFilters' store sells a 1.0 cubic foot catalytic carbon system on a 9 x 48 in tank for $886, rated for up to 10 ppm of iron and 10 ppm of hydrogen sulfide, needing at least 3.5 gpm to backwash
- **Price:** we could not confirm a current price for the 1.5 cu ft listing. [See it on Amazon (B00D0I7WN2)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00D0I7WN2)

Choose the larger bed if you run a high residual or a high peak flow. More carbon means more contact time for dechlorination.

**Watch for:** the absence of a published replacement interval. Carbon is spent when chlorine starts getting through, and nothing on the listing tells you when that will be.

> **Warning: protect the softener resin.** Chlorine oxidizes softener resin. Over months the beads swell, soften and break into fines, capacity falls and pressure drop rises. With continuous chlorination the carbon tank is the only thing standing between the chlorine and the resin, so:
>
> - **Plumb the carbon before the softener**, never after it and never bypassed while the chlorine pump runs.
> - **Test total chlorine at the carbon outlet** every month or two with a test kit that reads total, not just free, chlorine. Anything above zero means the carbon is spent or too small.
> - **Bypass the softener** whenever you shock chlorinate the well, and keep it bypassed until the chlorine is flushed out.
>
> How the damage happens, and the squeeze test that shows it, are in [how chlorine damages water softener resin](/blog/how-chlorine-damages-softener-resin/).

## What It Costs to Run

Chlorine use is small in volume. An illustrative house using 300 gallons a day at a 5 ppm dose feeds about 0.025 gallons of 6% bleach a day, about 9 gallons a year. Bleach is a modest line item. The running costs that add up are elsewhere:

- **Pump tubes.** The peristaltic tube wears and is replaced on a schedule set by the dial setting and run time.
- **Carbon media.** Replaced when chlorine breaks through, which a higher residual brings forward.
- **Test reagents.** A free and total chlorine kit, used weekly at first and monthly once stable.
- **Electricity and backwash water** for the filter and carbon tanks.

The hidden cost is time. Expect to mix solution, check the dial and test the residual every few weeks. If that routine will not happen, chlorine injection is the wrong system.

## Who Should Buy None of These

- **Clear water with coliform but no iron, sulfur or bacteria slime.** UV disinfection needs no chemicals and no carbon stage. Our separate guide to the best UV systems for well water covers it.
- **Iron under about 3 ppm with no iron bacteria.** A fine-mesh softener or an air injection iron filter is simpler. The choice is set out in [water softener for well water](/blog/water-softener-for-well-water/).
- **A rotten-egg smell from the hot taps only.** That is usually the water heater's anode rod, not the well.
- **A well that has never been shock chlorinated.** Shock it first and retest. If the problem does not come back, you do not need continuous treatment.
- **Anyone who cannot keep up the routine.** An unattended chlorine system either stops dosing or overdoses, and the softener pays for the second.

Size the pump on your chlorine demand and well-pump flow, size the contact tank on your peak flow, and never let chlorine reach the resin. For most homes that means the Stenner 45MHP10, a 120-gallon tank, an iron filter where the test calls for one, and a catalytic carbon tank ahead of the softener.
