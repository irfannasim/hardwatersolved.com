The **best acid neutralizers for well water** are matched to the starting pH. Between about 6.0 and 6.9, a calcite tank does the job. Below about 5.3, a soda ash feed pump replaces the tank. Every calcite option also adds hardness. On an example well at pH 6.0 that is up to about 5 gpg. The softener after it has to be sized for the water leaving the neutralizer, not the water in the test.

## Starting pH Decides the Media

![Graduated test tubes of water samples in a lab rack, the pH, alkalinity and hardness tests that decide which acid neutralizer a well needs](https://images.pexels.com/photos/7722830/pexels-photo-7722830.jpeg)

Neutralizer media differ in strength, and the stronger the media, the lower the pH it can correct. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/corrosive-water-problems) describes the tanks as filled with limestone or marble chips, magnesium oxide "or other alkaline material". It also notes that the treatment "will increase the hardness of the water, but this is necessary for proper corrosion control."

| Starting pH | Media | Hardness added per 1 mg/L of CO₂ neutralized | Sodium added | Upkeep |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| About 6.0 to 6.9 | Calcite (calcium carbonate) | up to 2.27 mg/L (0.13 gpg) | none | Top up calcite |
| About 5.3 to 6.0 | Calcite with Corosex (magnesium oxide) | between 1.14 and 2.27 mg/L, depending on the blend | none | Top up; Corosex can overshoot neutral |
| Below about 5.3, or no added hardness wanted | Soda ash (sodium carbonate) feed pump | none | about 1.05 mg/L | Mix solution, service pump, check pH |

*The per-milligram ratios come from the reaction chemistry. The 5.3 floor for blended media is the figure used in the site's guide to low-pH treatment; AFWFilters offers its blend "for pH levels less than 6".*

Two readings on your test set the amount: pH and alkalinity. Together they give the free carbon dioxide, which is the acid the neutralizer has to consume. How the two numbers interact, and why the same pH can mean very different treatment, is explained in [interpreting pH and hardness together](/blog/interpreting-ph-and-hardness-together/).

## The Second Problem: Sizing the Softener That Follows

This is where most neutralizer pages stop and most installations go wrong. A calcite bed turns acid into hardness, and the softener has to remove it.

![Best acid neutralizers for well water by starting pH: calcite adds 0.8 to 5.3 gpg, a Corosex blend adds 5.3 to 10.6 gpg, soda ash adds sodium, with softener regeneration days](/diagrams/acid-neutralizer-starting-ph-added-hardness-softener-load.svg "The lower the pH, the more hardness a calcite neutralizer hands to the softener")

The example below uses one well and changes only its pH. It has **8 gpg of hardness and 20 mg/L of alkalinity**, and four people use **300 gallons a day**. Carbon dioxide is estimated as 2 × alkalinity × 10^(6.0 − pH). The added hardness is the ceiling, as if every milligram of carbon dioxide were neutralized. That is the figure to design to before the system has run. Softener capacities are the Hellenbrand figures the site uses elsewhere: 22,930 grains for 1.0 cu ft at 6 lb of salt and 34,400 grains for 1.5 cu ft at 9 lb.

| Starting pH | Free CO₂ (est.) | Media | Hardness added (ceiling) | Design hardness | 1.0 cu ft: days between regenerations | 1.5 cu ft: days between regenerations |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raw well, no neutralizer | n/a | none | 0 | 8.0 gpg | 9.6 | 14.3 |
| 6.8 | 6.3 mg/L | Calcite | 0.8 gpg | 8.8 gpg | 8.7 | 13.0 |
| 6.5 | 12.6 mg/L | Calcite | 1.7 gpg | 9.7 gpg | 7.9 | 11.8 |
| 6.2 | 25.2 mg/L | Calcite | 3.4 gpg | 11.4 gpg | 6.7 | 10.1 |
| 6.0 | 40 mg/L | Calcite | 5.3 gpg | 13.3 gpg | 5.7 | 8.6 |
| 5.7 | 80 mg/L | Calcite + Corosex | 5.3 to 10.6 gpg | up to 18.6 gpg | 4.1 | 6.2 |
| 5.3 | 200 mg/L | Soda ash | 0 (about 210 mg/L sodium) | 8.0 gpg | 9.6 | 14.3 |

*Illustrative. Substitute your own pH, alkalinity and metered water use. Real neutralizers stop short of the ceiling as the water nears neutral.*

What the table tells you:

- **Down to about pH 6.5, the effect is small.** Under 2 gpg is added. A softener sized with a little margin copes without change.
- **At pH 6.0, the softener's work rises by two-thirds.** A 1.0 cu ft unit drops from nearly 10 days between regenerations to under 6. The 1.5 cu ft size is the safer choice.
- **On a pH 5.7 well, the blend sets the outcome.** At the top of its range it more than doubles the hardness. Soda ash avoids the hardness entirely and swaps it for sodium.
- **Salt rises with it.** On the 1.5 cu ft unit at 9 lb a regeneration, the raw 8 gpg uses about 230 lb of salt a year. At the pH 6.0 design figure of 13.3 gpg that becomes about 380 lb.

Two steps keep the softener right. First, fit a sample tap between the neutralizer and the softener. Second, after the neutralizer has run for two weeks, test hardness there and program the softener to that reading. The full step-by-step method, including what happens if the softener is set to the raw number, is worked through in [water softener vs acid neutralizer](/blog/water-softener-vs-acid-neutralizer/).

## The Flow Rating Sellers Bury

Neutralizers are sized on contact time. Water has to stay in the calcite long enough to dissolve what it needs. That is why AFWFilters rates a 1.5 cubic foot tank at only 3.2 gpm of service flow. A softener in the same 10x54 tank is rated at about twice that: AFWFilters quotes 6.5 gpm of service flow for its 1.5 cu ft fine-mesh softener.

The practical effect is a pH that looks fine in a slow test and falls during the morning rush. A shower, a dishwasher and a toilet refill together can pass 3.2 gpm. While they run, part of the water goes through with too little contact and reaches the copper still acidic.

Three ways to handle it:

- **Size up.** Choose the 20 size or a high-flow model when your measured peak is above the rating.
- **Measure peak, not average.** Time how long the house takes to fill a 5-gallon bucket with the busiest combination of fixtures running, or read the meter over a minute.
- **Test the outlet under load.** Take a pH reading at the sample tap while water is running hard, not after the line has been still.

## Six Numbers to Have Before You Order

Each pick above can be the right one or the wrong one depending on six figures. Collect them first:

| Number | Where it comes from | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| pH | Lab test of raw water | Calcite, blend or soda ash |
| Alkalinity | Same test | How much CO₂, so how much hardness is added and how fast media is used |
| Hardness | Same test | Whether a softener is needed after the neutralizer, and its size |
| Iron and manganese | Same test | Whether an iron stage is needed and which goes first |
| Peak household flow | Bucket test or meter reading | Tank size: 15 or 20 |
| Pump's sustained flow | Bucket test at an outside tap, timed over several minutes | Upflow or backwashing; whether a 4.4 gpm backwash is possible |

If the test is more than a year old, or was a strip test, order a lab panel. Strip readings are coarse, and near pH 6.0 a few tenths of a unit decide between plain calcite and a blend.

## Who Should Buy None of These

- **Wells with pH under about 5.3.** Even a blended bed adds large amounts of hardness there and wears quickly. A soda ash feed pump injected ahead of the pressure tank, as Penn State describes, is the usual answer, and it is a different purchase.
- **Households on a sodium-restricted diet, considering soda ash.** At pH 5.3 in the example above, soda ash could add about 210 mg/L of sodium. Treat drinking water separately, or discuss it with a doctor.
- **Radon or sulfur wells that will be aerated anyway.** Aeration strips carbon dioxide and raises pH. Retest pH after an aerator before buying a neutralizer.
- **Wells with iron bacteria or heavy iron.** Iron coats calcite and slime clogs it. Deal with those first, or put the iron filter first if its media tolerates your raw pH.
- **Anyone without a pH, alkalinity and hardness test.** Stained fixtures can come from copper or iron, and the fix differs.
