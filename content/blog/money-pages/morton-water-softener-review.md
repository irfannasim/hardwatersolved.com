**A Morton water softener review comes down to one question: does the salt saving hold up?** Partly. The Morton System Saver softeners are NSF/ANSI 44 efficiency-rated demand units, and the M34's manual rates it at 5,060 grains per pound of salt at its minimum dose. Against a timer softener or a conventional unit on a heavy salt dose, that saves several hundred pounds of salt a year. Against a well-set conventional metered softener, the gap is about 100 lb a year at best, roughly two and a half bags.

The bigger problem is availability. The Morton MSD34C shows as currently unavailable on Amazon, so this review also covers two in-stock alternatives from the same maker.

## Morton Water Softener Review: The Short Verdict

- **Salt efficiency:** genuinely good on paper. The lab rating sits well above the 3,350 grains-per-pound floor that NSF/ANSI 44 sets for an efficiency-rated softener.
- **Salt savings in practice:** large against timers and heavy doses, small against a modern metered softener set to an efficient dose.
- **Build and support:** NSF-certified for hardness reduction and efficiency. Parts are shipped to your home, but non-tank parts carry only a one-year warranty on the standard models.
- **Iron:** the M34 is rated for up to 9 ppm of clear-water iron, backed by WQA test data. Red-water and bacterial iron are out of scope.
- **Availability:** the MSD34C is listed on Amazon as unavailable. Buy only from stock you can see, and check that the seller still supports parts.

> **How this review was put together.** No softener was physically tested. The review uses the Morton System Saver manual for models M20 to M34, the M45C manual, the current NSF certification listing, and the salt-use formula from this site's guide to [how much salt a water softener uses](/blog/how-much-salt-does-a-water-softener-use/). The conventional reference figures come from the Hellenbrand ProMate 6.0 capacity table used in that guide. Each Amazon listing was opened to confirm the page exists.

## The M34 Spec Sheet, Normalised

A grain rating means nothing without the salt dose behind it, a point covered in detail in [advertised vs usable softener capacity](/blog/advertised-vs-usable-softener-capacity/). Morton's manual is unusually complete here. It gives capacity at three salt doses, so the salt efficiency at each one can be worked out:

| Morton M34 | Minimum dose | Middle dose | Maximum dose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salt per regeneration | 2.7 lb | 8.6 lb | 14.5 lb |
| Capacity | 13,700 grains | 29,200 grains | 34,900 grains |
| Grains removed per lb of salt | 5,060 (rated) | about 3,400 | about 2,400 |

Other figures from the same manual: 0.91 cubic feet of resin, a rated service flow of 7.8 gpm at a 14.7 psi pressure drop, up to 110 gpg of hardness, up to 9 ppm of clear-water iron, and 20 to 125 psi operating pressure.

Two things stand out. First, the "34" in the name is the maximum-dose capacity, so it is only reached at the least efficient setting. Second, the minimum dose is very small, 2.7 lb, so the softener regenerates often with little salt. That is where the efficiency rating comes from.

## The Salt-Savings Claim: A Worked Calculation

Morton markets its demand control, which it calls Look Ahead, as using salt and water only when needed. The honest way to test that is to compare annual salt use against the alternatives, at the same household and water. The formula is the one from the salt-use guide:

> **Pounds of salt a year = gallons a day × hardness (gpg) × 365 ÷ grains removed per pound of salt**

Worked for a family of four at 70 gallons per person per day (280 gallons a day) on 15 gpg water, the house needs 1,533,000 grains of hardness removed a year.

![Bar chart of annual salt use for a family of four at 15 gpg: Morton M34 at its best lab rating about 303 lb, conventional metered softener at 6 lb per cubic foot about 401 lb, at 15 lb about 712 lb](/diagrams/morton-water-softener-annual-salt-use-comparison.svg "The Morton saving is large against heavy doses and timers, small against an efficient metered unit")

What the chart shows:

- **Against a timer softener,** the saving is the biggest, because a timer regenerates on schedule whether hardness has been used or not. An illustrative timer set to 12 lb every fourth night burns about 1,095 lb a year, more than three times the M34's figure. Moving from a timer to any demand softener is the main win, and Morton's claim is fair on that comparison. The background is in [metered vs timer-based water softeners](/blog/metered-vs-timer-based-water-softeners/).
- **Against a conventional metered softener at a heavy dose,** the M34 at its rated efficiency uses about 409 lb less a year. That is around ten 40 lb bags.
- **Against a conventional metered softener set to an efficient 6 lb per cubic foot,** the gap falls to about 98 lb a year with the M34 at its best, and to about 18 lb with only the High Efficiency floor guaranteed.

## Why Your Real Salt Use Will Be Higher

The manual is candid about this. Rated efficiency is measured in the lab and represents "the maximum possible efficiency that the system can achieve". Operational efficiency, it says, is typically lower because of hardness, water use and other contaminants. Three things push your real figure up:

1. **The shipping setting.** The High Efficiency feature ships switched off. In that mode the softener uses its full rated capacity, and Morton describes it only as "most often" achieving maximum efficiency. Turn it on if salt cost is your priority. In California it must be on.
2. **The reserve.** A demand softener regenerates with some capacity still unused, so it does not hit the lab figure every cycle. The salt-use guide puts that overhead at around 11 percent in a worked example.
3. **Small households.** With little water use, a day override can force regenerations the hardness did not need. Salt use then follows the calendar, not the water.

The point of the chart is the gaps between rows, not the absolute numbers. Why some valves reach better grains-per-pound figures than others is explained in [what water softener salt efficiency means](/blog/water-softener-salt-efficiency/).

## What Morton Owners Replace First

The manual's troubleshooting section lists the causes of a softener that will not draw brine, from most to least likely. That list is a good guide to which parts wear first, because poor brine draw is the fault that turns soft water hard.

![Flow chart of the Morton water softener check order when it will not draw brine: salt bridge, nozzle and venturi, seating and gasket, valve seals, drain hose, brine valve and tubing](/diagrams/morton-water-softener-no-brine-draw-check-order.svg "Work down the list; most no-brine faults are found in the first two checks")

- **Salt bridges** are not a part failure, but the manual flags them first. High humidity or the wrong salt forms a crust, and the resin never gets brine. The fix is in [water softener salt bridge](/blog/water-softener-salt-bridge/).
- **The nozzle and venturi disc** is the most likely part to need attention. It is cheap and cleanable, and the manual gives a step-by-step cleaning procedure.
- **The gasket under the nozzle and venturi** deforms over time, so a cleaned disc may still leak air.
- **Valve seals** follow. Once they leak, brine draw and regeneration flows both suffer.

**Warranty.** The M20 to M34 manual covers the salt tank and fibreglass mineral tank for 10 years against rust, corrosion, leaks and bursting, and all other parts for one year. Parts are shipped free, but labour is not covered. On the M45C, Morton offers an extended warranty if you use its MWSC cleanser, and using any other softener additive voids that extension.

## Who Should Not Buy a Morton Softener

- **Anyone with red water, iron bacteria or more than about 9 ppm of iron.** The iron rating covers clear-water ferrous iron only. Wisconsin requires extra treatment above 5 ppm.
- **Households with high peak flow.** The M34's 7.8 gpm rating suits a modest home; several bathrooms in use at once call for a larger valve.
- **Buyers who want long parts cover.** One year on non-tank parts is short next to rivals that offer five years on the valve.
- **Anyone who wants to avoid salt.** None of these units remove the need for it. The trade-offs are covered in [salt-based vs salt-free water treatment](/blog/salt-based-vs-salt-free-water-treatment/).

The Morton salt claim is true where it matters most: a demand softener with a small minimum dose beats a timer or a heavy dose by a wide margin. Just do not expect it to halve the salt use of a conventional metered softener that is already set up well.
