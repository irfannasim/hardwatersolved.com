The **iron filter goes before the water softener**, and sediment control goes before both. The order is: pressure tank, coarse sediment screen, pH neutralizer if needed, iron filter, then softener. If you reverse it, the resin meets the iron first and fouls, and the iron filter downstream ends up treating water the softener has already been damaged by.

In practice, sediment and iron are one decision. The same wells tend to produce both. Both need to be out of the water before the resin. And both usually end up as backwashing tanks that share one pump, which leads to the scheduling problem at the end of this guide.

## Iron Filter Before Water Softener: The Order and What Each Stage Protects

| Position | Stage | What it protects |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pressure tank | Every stage after it, from pump surges |
| 2 | Coarse sediment screen or spin-down | Valve seals and pistons in every tank after it, from sand and grit |
| 3 | pH neutralizer (only if pH is low) | The iron filter, since many iron media need pH in a working range, and the plumbing |
| 4 | Iron, manganese or sulfide filter | The softener's resin, from iron coating and oxidized-iron clogging |
| 5 | **Water softener** | The house and water heater, from hardness |
| 6 | UV or point-of-use polishing | Drinking water |

![Well water treatment train showing pressure tank, sediment screen, pH neutralizer, iron filter, then water softener and UV in order](/diagrams/well-water-treatment-train-iron-filter-before-softener.svg "Each stage protects the one after it; the iron filter always sits ahead of the softener.")

Manufacturers put it the same way. Whirlpool's softener installation manual says "any other installed water conditioning equipment should be installed between the water inlet and water softener." Its installation diagram shows the sediment filter ahead of the softener and adds that the softener "will not remove sediment." Where the softener sits relative to the pressure tank and water heater is laid out in [where to install a softener on a well system](/blog/where-to-install-softener-on-well-system/).

**Why reversing it fails both ways.** If the softener comes first:

- Oxidized iron hits the resin and clogs it. Brine regeneration can't wash it out. Penn State Extension's [water softening fact sheet](https://extension.psu.edu/water-softening) states that "red-oxidized iron ... will clog the resin," and recommends that "filtration prior to softening" ensures oxidized iron is not processed by the softener.
- Dissolved iron takes up exchange sites and slowly coats the beads. Capacity drops cycle after cycle. See [water softener resin fouling](/blog/water-softener-resin-fouling/).
- The iron filter downstream now sees only what the fouled softener let through. You've paid for a filter that isn't protecting anything.

## Thresholds: When Each Stage Becomes Necessary

This is the table to check your water test against. Each row says what level triggers the stage and where that number comes from. Where there is no regulatory threshold, the table says so.

| Measured | Level | Stage it triggers | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Iron** | Below 0.3 mg/L | None for staining. EPA's secondary (aesthetic) guideline | Penn State Extension |
| **Iron, dissolved** | Up to your softener's rated limit (one manual rates 2 ppm ferrous) | Softener may carry it, with sizing compensation | Manufacturer manual |
| **Iron, dissolved** | Below 5 mg/L with pH above 6.7 | Upper end of what softening is suited for | Penn State Extension |
| **Iron + manganese** | About 3 to 10 mg/L | Oxidizing filter (such as manganese greensand) before the softener | Penn State Extension |
| **Iron** | Above 10 mg/L | Oxidation (chlorine, air) followed by filtration before the softener | Penn State Extension |
| **Iron, oxidized** | Any significant amount | Filter before the softener, whatever the total | Penn State Extension |
| **Manganese** | Above 0.05 mg/L | Treat with the iron stage. The health advisory is 0.3 mg/L | Penn State Extension |
| **pH** | Below 6.8 | Neutralizer ahead of a Birm-type iron filter | Clack Birm data sheet |
| **Hydrogen sulfide** | Any present | Remove before a Birm-type iron filter | Clack Birm data sheet |
| **Free chlorine** (if chlorinating) | At or above 0.5 ppm | Too high for Birm. Remove with carbon before Birm or resin | Clack Birm data sheet |
| **Sediment** | No regulatory number | Any visible sand or grit, or a well that has pumped sand before: fit a screen. Manufacturers recommend a filter ahead on rural supplies | Hellenbrand manual; Whirlpool manual |

The iron bands come from Penn State Extension's guidance on [iron and manganese in private water systems](https://extension.psu.edu/iron-and-manganese-in-private-water-systems). The Birm conditions come from Clack's [Birm media data sheet](https://www.cleanwaterstore.com/technical/water-treatment-manuals/Birm.pdf), which lists a pH range of 6.8 to 9.0, free chlorine under 0.5 ppm, hydrogen sulfide removed first, and dissolved oxygen at least 15% of the iron content. Other iron media (greensand, catalytic carbon, air-injection filters) have their own operating envelopes, so check the data sheet for the media you're buying.

Two notes on reading your result against this table:

- **"Dissolved" and "oxidized" are separate numbers.** A single "iron: 1.5 mg/L" doesn't tell you which row you're in. Ask the lab for filtered and unfiltered iron on the same sample. [Dissolved vs oxidized iron in well water](/blog/dissolved-vs-oxidized-iron-in-well-water/) explains the free glass test and the sampling trap.
- **Your softener's rating is the real ceiling, not the extension band.** If the manual says 2 ppm and your well has 4, you need the iron filter even though 4 is under Penn State's 5 mg/L. What a softener can realistically take is covered in [can a water softener remove iron?](/blog/can-a-water-softener-remove-iron/)

## Sediment First, but Not Too Fine

Sediment control goes first because sand is abrasive. It scores pistons and seals in every control valve after it. The Hellenbrand ProMate manual warns that on rural supplies unfiltered sand and sediment "may plug up the water softener restricting the flow through the resin bed," which "often requires rebedding." It recommends "an in-line filter system ahead of a water softener."

The trap is choosing a filter that is too fine. Here's how it plays out on an iron well:

1. Water leaves the well with dissolved iron and little oxygen.
2. In the pressure tank and piping, some of that iron starts to oxidize into fine rust particles.
3. A fine cartridge catches those particles, so it browns and clogs within days, and pressure drops through the house.
4. The iron filter behind it, which is built to trap oxidized iron and backwash it out, is left with nothing to do.

![Comparison of a fine sediment cartridge clogging with rust ahead of an iron filter versus a coarse screen placed before it](/diagrams/sediment-filter-too-fine-iron-well-clogging.svg "A fine cartridge ahead of an iron filter catches the iron the filter is built to remove.")

**What works instead:**

- **For sand and grit:** a coarse screen or spin-down filter that catches abrasive particles and leaves fine iron for the iron filter.
- **For heavy sediment:** a backwashing sediment filter tank, which cleans itself the way the iron filter does. It adds a third backwashing unit to the scheduling problem below, though.
- **For fine polishing:** if you want a fine cartridge, put it *after* the iron filter, where it only catches what the filter missed.

How to pick the micron rating and when to change the cartridge are covered in [when to replace a sediment prefilter before a softener](/blog/sediment-prefilter-before-a-softener/).

## The Backwash-Scheduling Conflict Between Two Units

This is the problem that trips up most DIY installs. It shows up weeks later as rusty water or a softener that fouls anyway, and nobody connects it to the clock.

**Why it happens.** Many controls leave the factory set to regenerate at the same time. The Hellenbrand manual states "the factory setting time is 2:00 a.m." The Whirlpool manual's setup screen also shows 2:00 AM as the default. Install an iron filter and a softener on the same day, leave both at their defaults, and they start regenerating together.

**Why it matters: flow.** Iron filters need a strong backwash to lift and clean a heavy media bed. The Clack Birm data sheet specifies a backwash rate of **10 to 12 gpm per square foot** of bed area. For common residential tank sizes, that works out to:

| Iron filter tank diameter | Bed area | Backwash needed (Birm) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 inch | about 0.55 sq ft | about 5.5 to 6.5 gpm |
| 12 inch | about 0.79 sq ft | about 7.9 to 9.4 gpm |
| 13 inch | about 0.92 sq ft | about 9.2 to 11.1 gpm |

The softener's drain flow is added on top of that. The Whirlpool manual lists a maximum drain flow of about 2 gpm for its units. So a 12-inch iron filter and a softener regenerating together can ask the well for 10 gpm or more, before anyone flushes a toilet. If your pump makes less than that, both backwashes run weak. Grit and iron stay in the iron filter's bed, the softener's bed doesn't lift properly, and both slowly foul.

**Why it matters: raw water.** Many control valves pass untreated water to the house while they regenerate, so check what your valve does in its manual. If the iron filter is backwashing and someone runs water, iron-laden raw water flows straight into the softener. If the softener is regenerating at the same time, its brine and rinse steps are using unfiltered water as well.

**The fix: stagger them, iron filter first.**

![Backwash schedule timeline showing iron filter and water softener clashing at the 2:00 a.m. default versus staggered at 1:00 and 3:00 a.m.](/diagrams/iron-filter-softener-backwash-schedule-stagger.svg "Run the iron filter first and the softener afterwards so they never backwash together.")

1. **Find each unit's total regeneration time** from its programmed cycle steps. Iron filters are often shorter, softeners longer.
2. **Set the iron filter earlier**, for example 1:00 a.m.
3. **Set the softener to start after the iron filter has finished and the pressure tank has recovered**, for example 3:00 a.m. The softener then regenerates on freshly filtered water.
4. **Keep both clear of the household's first water use in the morning.** A regeneration still running at 6:00 a.m. sends untreated water to the first shower.
5. **If a backwashing sediment filter is a third unit,** put it first (midnight, say), then the iron filter, then the softener.

The key-press sequences for changing regeneration time on common controls are in [how to manually regenerate a water softener](/blog/how-to-manually-regenerate-a-water-softener/). Check the clock on each unit after any power cut, because a control that resets to its default puts you back at 2:00 a.m. on both.

## Measure Your Pump Before Choosing the Iron Filter Size

The backwash table above explains why the iron filter should be chosen based on the pump, not only on the water test. Here's a quick check:

1. Run water until the pump starts, so you're measuring the pump and not the tank.
2. Open the tank-tee drain or the nearest hose bib fully and time how long it takes to fill a 5-gallon bucket.
3. Divide 300 by the seconds. That's gallons per minute. (20 seconds = 15 gpm. 40 seconds = 7.5 gpm.)

Compare that number with the iron filter's backwash requirement. If the pump can't supply it, a larger tank won't backwash properly. A smaller-diameter tank, a different media with a lower backwash rate, or a non-backwashing approach is a better fit. How to choose between an iron filter and a softener in the first place, or decide you need both, is in [water softener vs iron filter](/blog/water-softener-vs-iron-filter/).

## What Pretreatment Cannot Do

- **It doesn't make the water safe.** Sediment and iron filters are aesthetic and equipment-protection stages. Bacteria, nitrate and other health contaminants need their own tests and treatment.
- **It doesn't fix iron bacteria.** [Slimy, rust-coloured growth in toilet tanks](/blog/iron-bacteria-in-well-water/) means the well needs disinfection. A filter just becomes something else for the bacteria to coat.
- **It doesn't rescue resin that's already fouled.** If the softener ran for a year without pretreatment, fitting the iron filter stops things getting worse. Recovering the bed is a separate job. See [resin and iron cleaners](/blog/water-softener-resin-and-iron-cleaners/).
- **It doesn't stay right forever.** Well iron levels change. Retest every year and check the table against the new numbers.

To sum up: [sediment screen first, pH correction if needed, iron filter, then the softener](/blog/well-water-treatment-train-order/). Stagger their regeneration times so they never backwash together. That order, and a pump that can supply the backwash, is what keeps a softener on a well working for its full life.
