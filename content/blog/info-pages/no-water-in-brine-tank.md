**No water in the brine tank** can be perfectly normal. Softeners set to pre-fill sit nearly dry until about two hours before regeneration, every tank is briefly empty mid-regeneration after the brine is drawn, and in many tanks the water is simply hidden under the salt. A dry tank on a post-fill softener between cycles, though, means the refill has failed.

The difference comes down to timing, and a few minutes of checking settles which one you have.

## Is No Water in the Brine Tank Normal? Three Times It Is

Softeners make brine by letting water sit on salt. The question is *when* the water is added, and there are two designs.

**Post-fill (the traditional "wet" tank).** The valve refills the tank at the end of each regeneration. Water then sits under the salt for days, dissolving it into full-strength brine ready for the next cycle. On a Fleck 5600SXT downflow valve, the [service manual](https://www.pentair.com/content/dam/extranet/web/nam/fleck/manuals/42684-fleck-5600sxt-downflow-manual.pdf) lists brine refill as the last regeneration step, just before the valve returns to service.

**Pre-fill (the "dry" tank).** The valve adds water about two hours before the regeneration begins, lets it dissolve salt, draws it all out during the brine stage, and leaves the tank close to dry. The [Clack WS1 manual](https://www.clackcorp.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/V3115_WS1_1.25.pdf) describes both options: with pre-fill selected and regeneration set for 2 a.m., the fill happens at midnight.

Put side by side over a regeneration, the two look like this:

| Point in the cycle | Post-fill tank | Pre-fill tank |
|---|---|---|
| Days between regenerations | **A few inches of water under the salt** | **Dry, or nearly** |
| ~2 hours before regeneration | Water still there | Fills with water |
| Brine draw stage | Water drawn out, tank nearly empty | Water drawn out, tank nearly empty |
| Rinse stages | **Nearly empty** | **Nearly empty** |
| End of regeneration | Refills | Stays nearly empty |

**So a dry tank is normal:**

1. **Between cycles, on a pre-fill softener.**
2. **Mid-regeneration, on any softener,** after brine draw and before a post-fill refill.
3. **When the water is there but hidden under salt** — which is the most common "fault" of all.

**And it is a fault** when a post-fill tank is dry between cycles, or when a pre-fill tank is still dry in the hour or so before a scheduled regeneration.

**Why anyone builds a dry tank.** Salt sitting in water for days is what slowly breaks fine salt down into sludge, and a permanently damp pile is more prone to fusing, so keeping the tank dry between cycles is meant to reduce both. The trade-off is timing: the water gets only a couple of hours to dissolve salt before it is needed, rather than days. Neither design is better in every house, but they look completely different when you lift the lid — which is why owners who move from one softener to another, or buy a house with an unfamiliar unit, are the people most likely to mistake a correct dry tank for a broken one. If your softener was installed by a previous owner and there is no manual, the model number on the control head is usually enough to find one online.

## Look in the Right Place First

![Brine tank cutaway showing water hidden under the salt, visible only by shining a flashlight down the brine well beside the float](/diagrams/water-softener-brine-tank-cutaway-brine-well-water-level.svg "Look down the brine well, not at the salt: the water sits underneath.")

Most people who report no water in the tank are looking at the top of the salt. On a correctly filled tank, that is exactly what they should see.

**Salt belongs above the water.** The Fleck troubleshooting chart tells owners to maintain the salt level above the water level. A few inches of water under a foot or more of salt is invisible from the lid. Pushing a hand down through the salt only reaches the damp layer if your arm is long enough.

**Use the brine well instead.** The brine well is the vertical tube, usually in a corner of the tank, that houses the float and the brine line pickup. Its cap lifts off. Shine a flashlight straight down it and you can see the actual water surface, with no salt in the way. Measure from the top of the well with a ruler or a marked dowel.

**Also check for a bridge.** If the salt has fused into a crust above an air gap, the water underneath can be present but never touch the salt — or the probe can pass through an empty cavity and suggest a dry tank that is not. A broom handle pushed through the salt finds it in seconds; see [how to identify and break a salt bridge](/blog/water-softener-salt-bridge/).

**What you might find:**

| In the brine well | What it means |
|---|---|
| Several inches of water | Tank is wet — normal for post-fill |
| An inch or so, just covering the bottom | Low but present; watch after the next regeneration |
| Completely dry | Normal only for pre-fill between cycles or mid-regeneration |
| Water far above the salt | Opposite fault — see [brine tank full of water or overflowing](/blog/brine-tank-full-of-water-or-overflowing/) |

## The Timing Check That Separates Normal From Broken

If the brine well really is dry, find out whether it *should* be. This takes one of three routes, from quickest to most certain.

**1. Read the setting.** Your manual will say whether the valve refills before or after regeneration. On a Clack WS1, the refill option shows PoST (after the final rinse) or PrE (two hours before regeneration). Some other valves label the same choice as pre-fill, pre-brine or dry tank.

**2. Check the clock.** Note the regeneration time on the control (often 2 a.m.) and how many days until the next one. Then look in the brine well at two moments:

- **The morning after a regeneration.** Post-fill: water should be there. Pre-fill: dry is normal.
- **An hour before the next scheduled regeneration.** Both designs should have water by now if pre-fill is set; post-fill should have had water all along.

**3. Force the refill and watch it.** Start a manual regeneration and step the valve to the refill stage. On a Fleck 5600SXT, holding Extra Cycle for five seconds starts regeneration and each further press advances one step, with brine refill as step four. Then:

- **Time the refill** and watch water enter the brine well.
- **Measure the rise.** Roughly three pounds of salt dissolves in each gallon of water, so the refill volume is sized to the salt setting — a 9-pound setting needs about 3 gallons. In a common 18-inch round tank, a gallon is roughly an inch of depth, so expect a rise of a few inches.
- **Check it stops.** It should end when the programmed time runs out.

| What you see during forced refill | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Water enters, level rises a few inches, stops on time | Refill works — dry tank was a timing or design question |
| Water enters, then disappears over hours | **Leak** draining the tank |
| Trickle only, far less than expected | **Restricted** brine line or flow control |
| Nothing enters at all | **Blocked** line, closed float shutoff, or valve not reaching refill |
| Valve never reaches the refill step | **Control fault** — not regenerating or stuck |

## Six Causes of a Genuine Refill Failure

If the timing check says the tank should have water and does not, one of these is the cause. They are in order of how often they turn up and how easy they are to check.

**1. Blocked brine line flow control.** The brine line flow control is a small restrictor, usually in the brine elbow on the control valve, that meters water into the tank during refill. Sediment and salt debris block it. The Fleck troubleshooting chart lists insufficient water flowing into the brine tank as a cause of hard water, with the correction to check the brine tank fill time and clean the brine line flow control. With the unit bypassed and depressurised, unscrew the brine elbow, remove the flow control and rinse it clean.

**2. Refill time set too short or to zero.** After a power loss on some older controls, a reset, or someone changing the salt setting, the refill may be programmed far too short. Check the refill (fill, BF) setting against the salt dose. On controls where refill is calculated from the salt setting, a salt setting of zero or a tiny figure will produce almost no water.

**3. Float or shutoff jammed closed.** The safety float is designed to close the brine line when water gets too high. If it sticks in the raised position — salt crust on the rod, the float caught on the brine well wall, or a float assembly reassembled wrongly after a cleanout — it holds the line shut and no refill can pass. Lift the float assembly out and check it slides freely. How the float, brine well and grid fit together is covered in [how to clean a water softener brine tank](/blog/how-to-clean-a-water-softener-brine-tank/).

**4. Brine line kinked, crushed or disconnected.** The line between the valve and the brine well is soft tubing that gets pinched behind the tank, stepped on or pulled loose. Follow it end to end. An air check valve at the bottom of the brine well can also stick closed with salt; it should move freely.

**5. Valve not reaching the refill step.** If the softener is not regenerating at all, it never refills either. Equally, a valve that stops partway through a regeneration — after a power cut, or with a failed motor — can leave the tank empty after brine draw. Error codes and a display stuck on one step point here; see [water softener stuck in regeneration](/blog/water-softener-stuck-in-regeneration/).

**6. A leak draining the tank.** The refill works but the water does not stay. Check the floor around the tank for dampness, the tank walls for cracks (common after a winter in a cold garage), and the brine line fittings. An overflow fitting mounted too low will drain away water too.

**Also possible, less often:** very low inlet pressure, the bypass left partly closed so the valve has little water to work with, or a piston or seal fault that diverts refill water elsewhere.

## What a Dry Tank Does to Your Water

A genuine refill failure is not an emergency, but it is not harmless either.

**No brine, no recharge.** With no water to dissolve salt, the brine draw stage pulls air or nothing, and the resin is rinsed rather than regenerated. The display still logs a completed regeneration.

**Hardness returns over days, not hours.** The resin still has whatever capacity it had left, so soft water fades rather than stops. Penn State Extension's overview of [water softening](https://extension.psu.edu/water-softening) notes that the brine solution must be mixed and stored in the brine tank — without it, the exchange that softens water cannot be reversed.

**The salt level freezes.** Nothing dissolves, so the salt line stops moving. That is the same symptom a salt bridge or a clogged injector produces, and it is why a dry tank is one branch of the larger diagnosis in [why a water softener is not using salt](/blog/water-softener-not-using-salt/).

**Confirm with a test.** A hardness test at a cold tap tells you whether the softener has actually run out. The method is in [how to check if your water softener is working](/blog/how-to-check-if-water-softener-is-working/).

## A Stopgap While You Find the Fault

If you need soft water before the repair:

1. **Add water by hand** — a few gallons, enough to cover the grid plate or the bottom of the brine well by an inch or two.
2. **Wait at least two hours,** better four. The Clack manual advises waiting at least two hours after filling an empty tank with salt before regenerating, so the brine can reach strength.
3. **Run a manual regeneration.**
4. **Test the water afterwards.**

This works once or twice. It is not a fix. A tank that needs water carried to it every cycle has a refill fault that will not clear itself.

## Where This Check Runs Out

**It cannot tell you about brine strength.** A tank that fills correctly but sits in a cold space, or was refilled with salt only an hour before regeneration, can still make weak brine.

**It cannot find internal valve faults.** If the refill step runs, the line is clear, the flow control is clean and still no water arrives, the fault is inside the valve body — seals, spacers or the brine valve itself. That is where a parts diagram for your exact model, or a technician, becomes necessary.

**It does not replace the manual.** Refill behaviour varies by valve, firmware and the options the equipment maker chose. If your unit is not a Fleck or Clack, the principle is identical but the buttons and labels will differ.

## Diagnosing No Water in the Brine Tank, In Order

1. **Look down the brine well** with a flashlight, not at the top of the salt.
2. **Probe for a bridge** that might hide the water or the cavity.
3. **Find out whether your softener is pre-fill or post-fill** from the manual or the control.
4. **Check the tank the morning after a regeneration** and again an hour before the next.
5. **If it should be wet, force a refill** and time and measure it.
6. **No water enters:** clean the brine line flow control, check the float, trace the brine line.
7. **Water enters but disappears:** look for leaks and cracks.
8. **Valve never reaches refill:** work on the control.
9. **Check the refill time** against the salt setting.
10. **Stopgap if needed:** add water by hand, wait, regenerate, test.

No water in the brine tank is one of the few softener symptoms that is as likely to be correct as broken. The water is either hidden under the salt, waiting for a pre-fill, or gone because the refill has failed — and a flashlight and the regeneration clock tell those three apart before you need a single tool.
