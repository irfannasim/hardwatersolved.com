**Can you reuse water softener discharge?** Mostly no. Averaged over one regeneration, the discharge carries about 8,700 mg/L of chloride, roughly 25 times the level irrigation guidelines rate as severe, so it damages plants and soil. It is not greywater under state greywater laws. The only reuses that hold up are non-contact, non-agricultural ones, and none of them removes the salt.

That is a disappointing answer for people trying to waste less, and they deserve the evidence rather than a flat no. Here are the numbers, eight reuse ideas checked against them, and what actually works instead.

## What Is in the Water You Want to Reuse

The case turns on salinity, so start with a figure. Take a common softener: 1 cubic foot of resin regenerated with 6 lb of salt, sending about 50 gallons to the drain. The breakdown in [where water softener regeneration waste goes](/blog/where-water-softener-regeneration-waste-goes/) works out what that regeneration contains. From those figures:

| Component | Per regeneration | Averaged over 50 gallons |
|---|---|---|
| Chloride | about 3.6 lb | about **8,700 mg/L** |
| Sodium | about 0.85 lb | about 2,000 mg/L |
| Calcium and magnesium (as calcium) | about 1.3 lb | about 3,200 mg/L |
| Total dissolved salts | about 5.8 lb | about **14,000 mg/L** |

These are calculated averages, not lab results. The real concentration swings through the cycle. The backwash at the start is close to your raw water. The brine draw and slow rinse in the middle are far saltier than the average. The end of the fast rinse falls back toward fresh water.

## The Salinity Numbers Against Irrigation Standards

The reference most agronomists use for irrigation water is FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 29, *Water Quality for Agriculture*. Its [guidelines table](https://www.fao.org/4/t0234e/T0234E01.htm) sorts water into no restriction, slight to moderate, and severe, for salinity and for specific ions.

| Measure | FAO: no restriction | FAO: severe | Softener discharge (average) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total dissolved solids | under 450 mg/L | over 2,000 mg/L | about 14,000 mg/L, **7 times severe** |
| Chloride, surface irrigation | under 4 me/L (142 mg/L) | over 10 me/L (355 mg/L) | about 245 me/L, **25 times severe** |

![Log-scale chart of chloride: FAO no-restriction 142 mg/L, EPA aquatic 230, EPA taste 250, FAO severe 355, against about 8,700 mg/L in average softener discharge](/diagrams/softener-discharge-chloride-vs-irrigation-guidelines-chart.svg "Even the average discharge sits about 25 times above FAO's severe chloride line")

For comparison, EPA's secondary drinking water standard for chloride, set for salty taste, is 250 mg/L. EPA's chronic criterion for protecting freshwater aquatic life is 230 mg/L. The discharge is about 35 times the first and 38 times the second.

**How much fresh water would fix it?** To bring the average discharge down to FAO's no-restriction chloride level, each 50-gallon regeneration would need to be diluted about 60 times, into roughly 3,000 gallons of fresh water. At that point you are irrigating with fresh water that happens to contain a little brine, and the salt still accumulates in the soil.

**What about sodium?** It is less of a problem than people expect. The calcium and magnesium stripped off the resin travel with the sodium and partly offset its effect on soil structure. Salinity and chloride are what rule the discharge out. That is different from irrigating with softened tap water, where the calcium has been removed and the sodium effect on clay is the main concern. That case is covered in [should outdoor faucets bypass a water softener](/blog/should-outdoor-faucets-bypass-a-water-softener/).

## What Salt Does to Plants and Soil

High salinity harms plants in two ways, and the discharge does both.

- **Osmotic stress.** Salty soil water holds on to its water more tightly, so roots struggle to take it up. Plants wilt and stunt even when the soil is wet.
- **Specific ion toxicity.** Chloride is taken up by roots and builds up in leaves, causing burned edges and tips. Sprinkling salty water directly onto leaves makes it worse.

The FAO guidelines assume good drainage and enough rain or extra irrigation to leach salt below the root zone. Where rain is scarce, the salt from repeated doses stays in the soil and builds up season after season. The white crust in the photo above is what that looks like in its extreme form.

## Can You Reuse Water Softener Discharge? Eight Ideas Checked

| Idea | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Water the lawn or garden** | No | 7 times FAO's severe salinity line. Burns plants and salts the soil |
| **2. Route it into a greywater system** | No | Not greywater under state definitions, and it would salt the irrigation field |
| **3. Flush toilets with it** | Possible, rarely worth it | Non-contact, but needs storage and a permitted nonpotable system, and the salt still reaches the sewer |
| **4. De-ice the driveway** | No | About 1.4 percent salt against about 23 percent in road anti-icing brine, and it runs straight to the storm drain |
| **5. Kill weeds in gravel** | No | Works by salting the ground, which then drains to storm sewers or groundwater untreated |
| **6. Dust control on a gravel drive** | No | Same problem as weed killing |
| **7. Wash the car or siding** | No | Leaves salt spots and promotes corrosion |
| **8. Capture only the low-salt stages** | Possible in principle | Backwash and late rinse are close to fresh, but separating them needs timed valves |

### Why greywater rules do not cover it

Greywater laws define greywater by where it comes from, and softener discharge is not on the lists. California Water Code [section 14876](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=WAT&sectionNum=14876) includes wastewater "from bathtubs, showers, bathroom washbasins, clothes washing machines, and laundry tubs," and excludes kitchen sinks and dishwashers. Arizona's statutory definition in [ARS 49-201](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/49/00201.htm) covers water from "a clothes washer or a bathroom tub, shower or sink." A regeneration drain is none of these.

That matters practically. A greywater exemption or permit lets you irrigate with laundry or shower water under set conditions. It does not extend to brine, so diverting softener discharge into a laundry-to-landscape system takes it outside the rules. It would also defeat the purpose, because laundry greywater is already saltier than tap water, and adding brine would push it past what plants tolerate.

### The two cases that are at least possible

**Toilet flushing (idea 3)** is the one genuinely non-contact reuse. No one drinks the water and no plant receives it. The costs are real, though: a collection tank sized for 20 to 70 gallons per regeneration, a pump, backflow protection, and salty water sitting in toilet tanks, where it is harder on metal parts. Most plumbing codes treat this as a nonpotable water system that needs a permit. The saving is the regeneration water, perhaps a few thousand gallons a year. The chloride still reaches the sewer, because the toilet drains there. Ask your building department before building one.

**Capturing only the clean stages (idea 8)** makes sense on paper. On an iron-free supply the backwash is mostly raw water with some sediment. In practice all stages leave through one drain line, the timing varies with the valve's program, and on well water the backwash is exactly where iron and grit come out. It is rarely worth doing in a house.

## Why "Mostly No" Is Also the Law in Many Places

Leaving the chemistry aside, where softener discharge may go is usually regulated. Many local codes require it to go to a sewer or an approved septic system through an air gap. Some prohibit it from reaching storm drains, dry wells or the ground surface, because that bypasses all treatment. Some states restrict it from septic systems too. Massachusetts bars treatment-device backwash from on-site systems entirely.

So "can I reuse it" often becomes "can I discharge it anywhere except the sewer," and the answer to that is local. If you are on septic, the county health department decides. The practical detail for septic homes is in [water softener planning for a home with septic](/blog/water-softener-with-septic-system/).

## How Much Water Is Actually at Stake

It helps to put the volume in proportion before building anything to capture it. These figures are calculated for illustration.

| Situation | Regeneration water per year |
|---|---|
| Demand-initiated softener regenerating once a week at 50 gallons | about 2,600 gallons |
| Same softener regenerating twice a week | about 5,200 gallons |
| Timer softener regenerating every night at 50 gallons | about 18,000 gallons |

A household of four using 240 gallons a day indoors uses about 87,600 gallons a year. A weekly regeneration schedule is about 3 percent of that. One summer week of watering a 2,000 square foot lawn with an inch of water uses about 1,250 gallons, roughly half a year of efficient regenerations.

Two conclusions follow. For an efficient softener, the water at stake is modest, and a capture system for toilet flushing would save a few thousand gallons a year at best. For an inefficient timer unit, the waste is several times larger, and the cheapest fix is not reuse. It is regenerating less often. Moving from nightly to demand-initiated regeneration saves more water than any reuse scheme could capture, and it cuts the salt at the same time.

EPA WaterSense puts typical regeneration at 20 to 70 gallons per cycle, and up to 200 gallons for some larger systems, so check your own valve's program before assuming 50.

## What Actually Reduces the Waste

Reuse cannot take salt out of the discharge. Using fewer regenerations takes out both the salt and the water at once. That is the real answer for anyone trying to cut the waste.

1. **Set the hardness to your real test result.** An inflated setting triggers extra regenerations.
2. **Use a metered, demand-initiated valve.** A timer regenerates on schedule whether the resin is used up or not.
3. **Choose an efficient salt setting.** Lower salt doses remove more hardness per pound.
4. **Keep outdoor taps and irrigation off the softener.** Softening lawn water drives regenerations for nothing.
5. **Fix leaks and running toilets.** A metered softener counts every wasted gallon.
6. **Consider off-site regeneration or salt-free treatment** where brine is a real local problem. A portable exchange tank is regenerated at a central facility. A salt-free conditioner has no discharge at all, but it also does not remove hardness.

## What This Page Cannot Tell You

- **Your own discharge will differ.** A larger resin bed, higher salt setting or iron in the water all change the composition. A lab can test a sample if you want real numbers.
- **Local rules vary.** This page quotes two state greywater definitions. Your state and county may have their own wording on softener discharge.
- **No household device removes chloride from brine.** Any reuse moves the salt somewhere else. It does not make it disappear.

The well-intentioned instinct to reuse softener discharge points in the right direction, toward less waste. The way to act on it is at the brine tank, not the drain.
