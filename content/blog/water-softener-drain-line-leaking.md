A **water softener drain line leaking** usually has one of five causes: a loose or creeping clamp at the barbed fitting, a kink at the first elbow, an unsupported run that sags and splits, tubing gone brittle from sunlight or heat, or a blockage forcing water out of the weakest joint. Most are ten-minute fixes — as long as the repair keeps the air gap at the discharge end.

The catch is timing. The line only carries water during regeneration, so most drain leaks happen at night while nobody is watching.

## Where Along the Line Is It Leaking?

A drain line has four zones, and each one fails in its own way. Knowing which zone is wet halves the diagnosis.

| Zone | What is there | Typical leak | What it points to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. The valve outlet** | Drain elbow or fitting, the drain line flow control, an O-ring and a clip | Drip at the elbow during backwash | Elbow O-ring, clip not seated, cracked fitting |
| **2. The first connection** | Tubing pushed over a barb and clamped, or a compression nut and insert | Seep or spray at the tube end | Clamp creep, tubing not fully on the barb, split tube end |
| **3. The run** | Tubing along the wall, floor or ceiling to the drain | Wet line along the floor, drip from a low point | Kink, sag and split, chafe, UV or heat embrittlement |
| **4. The discharge end** | Air gap device, standpipe, floor drain or laundry tub | Water on the floor near the drain | Overflow, splash, blocked receptor, tubing pushed down into the drain |

**How to find it quickly.** Run a manual regeneration during the day and walk the line during the backwash stage, which normally has the highest flow. Dry the line first and wrap any suspect joint in paper towel so you can see the first wet spot, not where the water ended up. If the wet spot is right at the control valve rather than on the tubing, it may be a head leak instead — the dry-and-mark method for that is in [why a water softener leaks at the control head](/blog/water-softener-leaking-at-control-head/).

**One thing that is not a leak.** Water running to drain between regenerations, with the line cold and flowing at 3 in the afternoon, is a valve fault, not a drain line fault. That is covered in [why a water softener is constantly draining](/blog/water-softener-constantly-draining/).

## Water Softener Drain Line Leaking at the Fitting: Clamp Creep

![Close-up of a metal pipe clamp on a stainless steel surface, the type of band clamp that holds softener drain tubing on its barb and slowly loses grip as the plastic creeps](https://images.pexels.com/photos/8853524/pexels-photo-8853524.jpeg)

The joint between the tubing and the barbed fitting on the valve is the single most common drain leak. It was tight when installed. Two years later it drips.

**What clamp creep is.** Flexible plastic tubing under constant squeeze from a clamp slowly flows away from the pressure point. It thins under the band and the clamp loses tension, even though nobody has touched the screw. Temperature changes speed it up — the line fills with cool water during a regeneration, then sits dry and warms up in between, over and over. The joint that sealed at installation ends up only just touching the barb.

**The signs:**

- The clamp turns freely by hand, or can be slid along the tube
- The tube end has a visible groove or waist where the clamp sits
- The drip starts during backwash, when back pressure in the line is highest
- The tube can be twisted on the barb

**The fix, done properly:**

1. **Wait for the unit to be in service,** so there is no flow. You do not need to bypass for a drain line repair unless you are working on the valve itself.
2. **Cut off the last inch of tubing** with a sharp utility knife or tube cutter, square to the tube. Old tubing that has been clamped and creeping is deformed; reusing it rarely seals.
3. **Warm the new end** in hot tap water for a minute if it is stiff, then push it fully onto the barb, right to the shoulder of the fitting.
4. **Fit a new clamp.** A stainless steel band clamp or the clamp the manufacturer supplies. Tighten firmly, but stop before it cuts into the tubing.
5. **Check again in a week** and after the next few regenerations. Clamps settle.

**If the valve uses a compression nut instead of a barb,** the fix is different. Clack, for example, supplies a nut and polytube insert for 5/8-inch flexible tubing on its drain elbow, and its manual says to slide the nut on, insert the polytube insert into the tube end, and tighten onto the fitting. If you are connecting rigid pipe to a threaded drain fitting, Clack specifies Teflon tape on the threads and no pipe dope ([Clack WS1 service manual](https://www.clackcorp.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/V3115-99-WS1-1.25-DRAWINGS-AND-SERVICE-MANUAL.pdf)).

**If the leak is at the elbow itself,** not the tube, the drain fitting on many valves is pushed into the valve body, sealed with an O-ring, and held with a clip. A new O-ring and a fully seated clip usually fix it.

## Failure Modes Specific to Drain Tubing

![Coiled lengths of corrugated plastic tubing, the flexible kind of line that kinks at tight bends and flattens when a softener drain run is pulled around a corner](https://images.pexels.com/photos/36449552/pexels-photo-36449552.jpeg)

Drain tubing lives a harder life than most household plumbing: long periods empty, short bursts of high flow, often loose on the floor, sometimes near a window or a water heater. It fails in ways a supply pipe does not.

| Failure | What causes it | How to recognise it | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **UV embrittlement** | Sunlight through a basement or garage window, or outdoor runs | Tubing turns chalky, faded, cloudy or stiff; cracks when bent; hairline splits on the sunlit side | Replace the run; reroute out of direct light or sleeve it in opaque pipe |
| **Kinking at the elbow** | Tubing pulled tight around the first bend off the valve, or pushed against a wall | Flattened or white-creased section; split along the crease; weak backwash flow | Replace the section; use a gentle radius or a rigid elbow fitting |
| **Sag and split** | Long unsupported runs; water sits in the low point between cycles | A belly in the line; drip from the lowest point; tubing stretched thin there | Replace the section; support it at regular intervals with a steady fall |
| **Heat damage** | Tubing touching a water heater flue, furnace or hot pipe | Softened, distorted or discoloured patch | Reroute away from heat; replace the damaged length |
| **Chafe** | Tubing rubbing on a concrete edge, joist or bracket as it moves during backwash | Worn flat spot, then a hole | Clip the line still; protect sharp edges |
| **Freezing** | Runs through unheated garages, crawl spaces or outdoors | Split after a cold snap | Reroute inside heated space; replace |

**Why the elbow kink matters twice.** A kink is a leak waiting to happen, and it is also a restriction. The valve cannot tell the difference between a kink and a clogged line; it just sees back pressure. Clack's troubleshooting table lists "restricted or kinked drain line" among the causes of excess water in the brine tank, and "drain line restriction or debris cause excess back pressure" among the causes of the control failing to draw brine. A softener that stops using salt and has a drain leak at the same time is often suffering from a single kink.

**Why sag is worse than it looks.** A sagging line never fully empties. The water that sits in the low point between cycles adds weight, pulls the belly lower, and keeps the tubing stressed in one spot. That is where it thins and splits.

## When a Blocked Line Causes the Leak

Sometimes the leak is a symptom and the real fault is further downstream. When flow cannot get out the end fast enough, pressure builds in the line and water escapes at the weakest joint — usually the barb at the valve.

**Common blockages:**

- **Scale and debris** in old tubing, especially on iron-bearing or very hard water
- **Resin beads or fines** carried to drain from a damaged underdrain or backwash that is too strong
- **A drain line that is too small for the length.** Both Fleck and Clack specify a 1/2-inch minimum for residential valves and require 3/4-inch for runs over 20 feet or backwash flows above 7 gpm ([Fleck 5600SXT service manual](https://www.pentair.com/content/dam/extranet/web/nam/fleck/manuals/42684-fleck-5600sxt-downflow-manual.pdf)). A long run reduced to a smaller tube is effectively a permanent restriction.
- **A line that climbs too high.** Clack lists "drain line too long or too high" as a cause of failing to draw brine. Overhead drain runs are allowed on many valves, but they have limits in the manual for your valve.
- **The drain line flow control itself.** It sits inside the drain elbow and can be partly blocked by debris; Clack's manual says it can be cleaned in dilute sodium bisulfite or vinegar, or replaced, and warns against using a wire to clear it.

**Test for a blockage.** Disconnect the tubing at the discharge end and hold it into a bucket during backwash. A strong, steady stream means the line is clear. A weak or spluttering flow means a restriction somewhere between the valve and the end.

## Repairing the Line Properly

For anything more than a clamp, replacing the whole run is usually better than splicing. Drain tubing is inexpensive, and old tubing that has kinked or embrittled in one place is rarely healthy everywhere else.

1. **Wait until the softener is in service** and note when the next regeneration is scheduled.
2. **Measure the existing run** and add a little for a clean route with gentle bends.
3. **Match or exceed the size** of the valve's drain fitting and the manual's minimum. Never step down.
4. **Choose the tubing for the route.** Flexible polyethylene or vinyl tubing for short runs; rigid PVC or PEX for long runs, overhead runs, or anywhere flexible tubing would sag or be knocked.
5. **Connect at the valve** with a fresh end and a new clamp, or the compression nut and insert.
6. **Route it with no tight bends.** If the drain is behind the unit, the drain elbow on some valves can rotate — Clack's elbow rotates 180 degrees — which is better than forcing tubing into a U-turn.
7. **Support it along its length** so it has a steady fall to the drain and no low points.
8. **Terminate it correctly** — see the air gap section below — and secure the end so it cannot whip out during backwash.
9. **Run a manual regeneration** and watch the line through backwash and rinse.

**On splicing.** If you must join tubing, use a barbed coupling sized for the tube and a clamp on each side, placed on a straight, supported section. Never splice at a bend or a low point.

## Support Spacing and Routing That Prevent a Repeat

The repair lasts as long as the route does. Most drain lines split because of how they were run, not because the tubing was bad.

**Support spacing.** There is no universal figure for softener drain tubing, and supports for plastic pipe are set by the hanger tables in whichever plumbing code your area adopted. As a practical rule, flexible drain tubing is soft enough that support every two to three feet on horizontal runs prevents sag, with supports close to both ends and either side of any bend. Rigid pipe can span further; follow the hanger table in the code edition your jurisdiction adopted.

**Routing rules that prevent every failure in the table above:**

- **Keep a steady fall** from the valve to the drain wherever possible, so the line drains empty between regenerations
- **Use gentle bends,** not tight loops; if a corner is tight, use a rigid elbow fitting
- **Keep it out of sunlight** — away from windows, or sleeved in opaque conduit
- **Keep it away from heat** — flues, water heaters, furnaces, hot pipes
- **Clip it so it cannot move** during backwash surges, and pad sharp edges
- **Keep it in heated space,** or it can freeze and split
- **Respect the valve's length and height limits** — check the manual

## Keep the Air Gap When You Repair It

![Water pouring from a pipe outlet into a concrete basin below through open air, illustrating the visible gap a softener drain line needs between its end and the receiving drain](https://images.pexels.com/photos/37117678/pexels-photo-37117678.jpeg)

This is where a leak repair can create a bigger problem. The discharge end of a softener drain line is not just an exit; it is the barrier that stops drain water being drawn back into the softener and into your supply.

**What the manufacturer requires.** Fleck's 5600SXT manual states: "You must have an air gap on the drain line to prevent back flow of drain water into the system," and specifies an air gap of twice the drain line pipe diameter, with a minimum of 1 inch. That means the end of the tubing sits above the flood rim of the receptor, with open air between them.

**What codes generally require.** Model plumbing codes in the U.S. generally treat water softener discharge as indirect waste that must discharge through an air gap or air break into a trapped and vented receptor such as a standpipe, laundry tub or floor drain — not piped directly into a drain line. The specific requirements, including whether an air break is acceptable, the minimum gap, and the receptor types allowed, vary between codes and between editions, and many jurisdictions add local amendments. **Check the edition your jurisdiction adopted,** or ask your local building department, before relying on any particular figure.

**Common repairs that break the air gap:**

| What people do | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Push the tubing further down the standpipe to stop splashing | The end sits below the flood rim; no air gap |
| Tape or seal the tubing into the drain opening to stop a leak | Creates a direct connection |
| Connect the line to a sink tailpiece or drain pipe with a fitting | Direct connection, unless using a listed device designed for it |
| Let the end drop into the floor drain grate | End can be submerged if the drain backs up |

**If the end splashes or overflows,** the fix is at the receptor, not by shoving the tube lower. Clip the tube in place above the rim, use a listed air gap fitting designed for softener drains, or make sure the receptor is large enough and clear enough for the backwash flow. A standpipe or laundry tub that overflows during backwash is a drain capacity problem; the leak you see on the floor is the receptor, not the tubing.

## Testing the Repair

![Water droplets scattered across a metal drain surface, the kind of residual wetness to check for at the discharge end after a softener drain line repair](https://images.pexels.com/photos/29830349/pexels-photo-29830349.jpeg)

1. **Dry the whole line and every joint.**
2. **Run a manual regeneration** during the day.
3. **Watch the backwash stage** from start to finish; it is usually the highest flow.
4. **Check the discharge end:** a steady stream, no splashing out of the receptor, and a visible air gap.
5. **Wrap the valve connection in paper towel** and check it at the end of the regeneration.
6. **Check it again after a week.** Clamps settle and tubing relaxes.
7. **Then add it to your routine** — a look along the drain line once or twice a year fits naturally into the [water softener maintenance schedule](/blog/water-softener-maintenance-schedule/).

## What This Repair Will Not Fix

- **Water running to drain constantly.** That is an internal valve leak, not a line fault.
- **A softener that has stopped using salt** because of a failing injector or seal. A kinked or blocked drain line can cause poor brine draw, but so can several other things — see [why a water softener is not using salt](/blog/water-softener-not-using-salt/).
- **A leak at the control head.** Different parts, different repair.
- **Receptor or house drain problems.** If the standpipe or floor drain itself backs up, the softener is just the first thing to reveal it.

A water softener drain line leaking is almost always cheap to fix and easy to prevent. Replace what is damaged, support the run so it cannot sag, keep it out of light and heat, and leave the air gap exactly where it belongs — above the drain, with open air in between.
