A **water softener constantly draining** is almost always one of two faults: the control valve is stuck in a regeneration position such as backwash or rinse, or the valve has returned to service but worn seals, spacers or a fouled piston let water leak past into the drain port. Put the unit in bypass to stop the waste, then manually advance the valve — if the flow stops, it was stuck; if it doesn't, the seals are leaking.

Before the diagnosis, though, it is worth knowing what this is costing, because the number is usually bigger than people expect and it changes how urgently you should act.

## What a Water Softener Constantly Draining Actually Costs

![Woman working through household bills with a pencil and calculator, the calculation that turns a softener drain flow rate into a monthly water and sewer cost](https://images.pexels.com/photos/7680742/pexels-photo-7680742.jpeg)

A drain line that runs around the clock is the most expensive softener fault there is. The water goes straight from the supply, through the valve and into the sewer or septic system, and every gallon passes through your water meter on the way.

The arithmetic is simple: **gallons per minute × 1,440 minutes = gallons per day.**

| Drain flow | Per day | Per 30 days | At $5 per 1,000 gal | At $10 per 1,000 gal | At $15 per 1,000 gal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 gpm (a slow trickle) | 144 gal | 4,320 gal | $22 | $43 | $65 |
| 0.5 gpm | 720 gal | 21,600 gal | $108 | $216 | $324 |
| 1.0 gpm | 1,440 gal | 43,200 gal | $216 | $432 | $648 |
| 1.5 gpm | 2,160 gal | 64,800 gal | $324 | $648 | $972 |
| 2.0 gpm | 2,880 gal | 86,400 gal | $432 | $864 | $1,296 |
| 3.0 gpm | 4,320 gal | 129,600 gal | $648 | $1,296 | $1,944 |

The per-1,000-gallon rates are illustrative, not quoted from any utility. Many water bills charge sewer on metered water as well, so the rate that matters is **water plus sewer combined**. Find both lines on your last bill, divide by the gallons billed, and use that number.

**For context.** The EPA's WaterSense programme puts normal softener regeneration at [up to 10,000 gallons a year](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/cation-exchange-water-softeners), and estimates that an average family's household leaks waste around [9,400 gallons a year](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/statistics-and-facts). A softener stuck at 1.5 gpm passes both of those figures in under five days.

**On a private well** there is no water bill, but the cost does not disappear. The pump runs far more than it was sized to, and a septic system receives thousands of extra gallons a day it was never designed to absorb. That hydraulic load is the more serious risk than the electricity.

**Why the trickle row matters.** Most people only notice full backwash flow, the obvious rush of water. A worn seal leaking a tenth of a gallon a minute is nearly silent and easy to miss for months, and it still adds up to more than 50,000 gallons a year.

## Measure Your Own Drain Flow in Five Minutes

![Hand holding a smartphone running a stopwatch app, the simple timing tool needed to measure how fast a softener drain line is flowing](https://images.pexels.com/photos/4114789/pexels-photo-4114789.jpeg)

You do not need to guess where on that table you sit. Two methods, both free.

**Method 1: the timed container.**

1. **Find the end of the drain hose.** It usually terminates at a floor drain, standpipe or laundry sink with an air gap above the drain opening.
2. **Hold a one-gallon jug or marked bucket under it** and start a stopwatch.
3. **Stop the watch when it reaches one gallon.**
4. **Divide 60 by the seconds.** Forty seconds per gallon is 1.5 gpm. Two minutes per gallon is 0.5 gpm.

For a slow trickle, use a measuring jug and time how long it takes to collect a quart, then multiply accordingly.

**Method 2: the house water meter.** Useful if the drain hose is awkward to reach, or to confirm the softener is the only thing using water.

1. **Turn off every fixture** and make sure nothing is running: no dishwasher, washing machine, irrigation or ice maker fill.
2. **Read the meter**, wait exactly ten minutes, and read it again.
3. **Put the softener in bypass** and repeat the ten-minute reading.
4. **The difference is the softener's drain flow**, and any movement left over in bypass is a separate leak somewhere else in the house.

**What the number tells you straight away.** Every control valve has a drain line flow control, a small restrictor that sets the backwash rate for the tank size. Clack's WS1 service manual, for example, lists drain line flow controls from 0.7 to 10 gpm for its standard 3/4" drain fitting, with residential tanks at the low end of that range. A measured flow close to the rate stamped on your restrictor means the valve is fully open to drain — a position problem. A flow far below it, a steady trickle, points more towards a seal leak.

## Confirm It Is the Softener and Not the Brine Tank

![Square stainless steel floor drain set in white tiles, the kind of floor drain where both a softener drain hose and a brine tank overflow line often discharge](https://images.pexels.com/photos/5768318/pexels-photo-5768318.jpeg)

Two different hoses can deliver water to the same floor drain, and they point to different faults.

| Which hose is running | Where it connects | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Drain line** | The control valve head, usually a small elbow fitting on top or at the back | The control valve is sending water to drain — carry on with this article |
| **Brine tank overflow** | A barbed fitting on the side of the brine tank, below the lid | The brine tank is overfilling and spilling. A different fault with a different fix |

**Follow the wet hose back to its source.** It takes thirty seconds and prevents a lot of wasted effort. If it is the overflow, the causes are refill control, the safety float and brine draw, all covered in [why a brine tank is full of water or overflowing](/blog/brine-tank-full-of-water-or-overflowing/).

**Both at once is possible.** A valve stuck in its brine refill position keeps pushing water into the brine tank until the safety float closes or the overflow takes it. If the drain line and the overflow are both running, the valve position is the root cause and the overflow is the symptom.

**While you are there, look at the drain hose itself.** A kinked, crushed or partly blocked drain hose causes backwash and brine draw problems, and a leaking connection on that hose is a separate issue worth reading about in [why a softener drain line leaks](/blog/water-softener-drain-line-leaking/). Neither makes the valve run continuously on its own.

## Stop the Waste Before You Diagnose Anything

This is the step people get wrong, because the instinct is to pull the plug.

**Unplugging does not reliably stop the flow.** If the valve has stalled in a backwash or rinse position, cutting the power simply freezes it in that position, and water keeps flowing to the drain under mains pressure. Pentair's [Fleck 5600SXT service manual](https://www.pentair.com/content/dam/extranet/web/nam/fleck/manuals/42683-fleck-5600sxt-upflow-manual.pdf) is explicit: if power fails during regeneration, the valve stays in its current position until power is restored.

**Use the bypass valve instead.** It sits at the back of the control head: a single lever, a push-pull knob, or a set of three valves on older installations. In bypass, the softener is isolated from the supply, the drain stops, and the house gets untreated water in the meantime.

**Move it slowly.** A bypass that has not been operated in years can stick, and forcing it tears O-rings. Work it back and forth gently if it resists.

**Leave the power connected for now.** You need the control powered for the diagnostic test, and most units will not advance through their cycle without it.

## The One Test That Splits the Diagnosis

The whole diagnosis turns on a single question: **does moving the valve to its service position stop the drain flow?**

**Step 1 — read the display.** Is the control showing a regeneration step, a countdown, a cycle number such as "C2", a flashing indicator, or an error code? Or does it show the normal time of day, as if it is in service?

**Step 2 — take it out of bypass briefly** so there is pressure at the valve, and confirm the drain is still running.

**Step 3 — manually advance the valve.** How depends on the control:

| Control | How to advance a regeneration step |
|---|---|
| Fleck 5600SXT and similar SXT timers | Press the Extra Cycle button once per step; each press advances to the next cycle position |
| Autotrol Logix 740/760 | Hold SET, then press SET and UP together to advance one cycle; holding SET and UP for five seconds cancels the regeneration and returns the camshaft home |
| GE GXSH40V-type controls | Press RECHARGE to move from one stage to the next |
| Mechanical dial timers | Turn the manual regeneration knob slowly in the direction marked on the dial |

Those button sequences come from the manufacturers' own manuals. For any other model, look for a "manual advance" or "manual regeneration" section in your owner's guide.

**Step 4 — step all the way back to service and wait a minute.** Then check the drain.

**What the result means:**

| What happens | Diagnosis | Go to |
|---|---|---|
| Valve advances, reaches service, drain stops | **Stuck position** — something stopped the valve advancing on its own | Branch A |
| Valve will not advance at all, or display shows an error | **Stuck position** with a drive or control fault | Branch A |
| Valve reaches service, display looks normal, drain keeps flowing | **Internal leak** — seals, spacers, piston or a disc not seating | Branch B |
| Drain flow changes in every position but never stops | **Internal leak**, usually a badly worn seal stack | Branch B |

## Branch A: The Valve Is Stuck in a Cycle Position

If advancing the valve by hand stopped the flow, the valve itself can still seal — it just could not get to service without your help. The question is what stopped it.

**The usual culprits, roughly in order of likelihood:**

- **A power interruption mid-cycle.** The simplest explanation, and one that leaves no permanent fault. If it happened once after a storm and the valve now completes its cycle normally, you are done.
- **Motor failure.** No hum and no movement when the control calls for a step change.
- **A stripped or broken drive gear.** The motor runs, but the cam or piston does not move.
- **A position switch or optical sensor fault.** The valve moves but the control does not know it has arrived, so it either stops early or keeps hunting.
- **Iron, manganese or grit binding the piston.** The motor is working against a piston that will not slide. Fleck's error code 0 exists for exactly this: the drive took longer than six minutes to reach the next position.

**Test it properly after the manual reset.** Start a manual regeneration and watch the whole cycle. If it stalls again at the same step, the fault is real and repeatable.

The full component-by-component isolation sequence — listening to the motor, watching the cam and checking the sensor, plus the point at which forcing a valve starts breaking it — is in [why a water softener is stuck in regeneration](/blog/water-softener-stuck-in-regeneration/). If the control is showing a code rather than a step number, decode it first with [the water softener error code reference](/blog/water-softener-error-code-or-blank-display/).

## Branch B: Worn Seals, Spacers or a Fouled Piston

If the valve is in service and water still reaches the drain, something inside the valve body is no longer separating the flow paths. How that happens depends on what kind of valve you own.

| Valve design | Common examples | What wears | Typical repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Piston and seal stack** | Fleck 5600, 7000; Clack WS1 | Rubber seals and plastic spacers the piston slides through; the piston itself scores or fouls | Seal-and-spacer kit, often with a new piston |
| **Disc valves with springs** | Autotrol 255 / Performa | Valve discs and their return springs; debris under a disc | Clean or replace discs; replace weak springs |
| **Rotor and disc** | Many retail cabinet units, including GE and Whirlpool models | Rotor seal and disc face, wave washer | Rotor and seal kit |

**What the manuals say.** Fleck's troubleshooting table lists "internal control leak — change seals, spacers, and piston assembly" and "foreign material in control" among the causes of continuous drain flow. Autotrol's lists "drain control clogged with resin or other debris" and "valve stem return spring weak" for water flowing or dripping at the drain after regeneration.

**Why seals fail.** Age is the main one. Chlorine in city water is an oxidiser, and rubber parts exposed to it for years gradually lose their elasticity. Iron and manganese coat the piston and spacers, which then tear the seals as they slide. Sand or pipe scale after plumbing work can lodge in the seal stack and hold it open.

**The honest limit of a DIY seal job.** On a Fleck or Clack piston valve, the piston and seal-kit swap is well documented and needs no special tools beyond care and cleanliness, but spacers must go back in the correct order and orientation, and lubricants must be the silicone type the manufacturer specifies. Petroleum grease swells the seals. If you are not comfortable taking the head apart, this is exactly the kind of job covered in [when to call a water softener technician](/blog/when-to-call-a-water-softener-technician/).

**Clean the cause, not just the parts.** If the old piston came out coated in orange or black deposits, new seals will fail the same way. That points to iron or manganese in the feed water, and a regular resin and valve cleaning routine — see [water softener resin and iron cleaners](/blog/water-softener-resin-and-iron-cleaners/).

## What a Constantly Draining Softener Cannot Tell You

A few limits worth stating plainly, because they save wasted money.

- **Constant draining is not caused by the salt.** Salt bridging, mushing and the wrong salt grade cause soft-water failures, not a running drain.
- **It is not caused by the resin.** Resin fouling or channeling degrades softening; it does not open a drain path.
- **High water pressure makes a leak worse, not a new fault.** Pressures well above the valve's rating accelerate seal wear, and a pressure-reducing valve protects the new parts. It does not fix a leak that already exists.
- **A water bill spike is not proof on its own.** Toilets with failing flappers, irrigation leaks and running hose bibs are all more common than softener faults. The ten-minute bypass meter test above is what separates them.

## The Sequence, In Order

1. **Measure the drain flow** with a jug and a stopwatch, and convert it to a monthly cost using your own water-plus-sewer rate.
2. **Confirm which hose is running** — control valve drain line or brine tank overflow.
3. **Put the softener in bypass** to stop the waste. Do not rely on unplugging it.
4. **Read the display:** regeneration step, error code, or normal service.
5. **Manually advance the valve** step by step back to service, with pressure restored.
6. **Flow stops at service:** stuck position. Run a full manual regeneration and watch where it stalls.
7. **Flow continues at service:** internal leak. Seals, spacers, piston, disc or rotor, depending on valve design.
8. **Look at what came out** — deposits on the piston tell you whether iron or sediment will destroy the new seals too.
9. **After repair, re-measure** with the meter test to confirm the drain is fully closed in service.

A water softener constantly draining is loud about its cost and quiet about its cause, which is why the order matters. Stop the flow at the bypass, then let one manual advance tell you whether you are chasing a stuck valve or a worn seal — the rest of the repair follows from that answer.
