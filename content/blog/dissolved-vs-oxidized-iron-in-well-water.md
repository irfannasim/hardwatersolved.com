The difference between **dissolved vs oxidized iron** shows up in a glass of cold water within 30 minutes. Water that comes out clear and turns orange as it stands carries dissolved (ferrous) iron. Water that is already tinted or specked at the tap carries oxidized (ferric) iron. A softener can only treat the first kind. The second needs filtration.

That test costs nothing and it decides which equipment is worth considering. A lab confirms it, but the lab result has a trap of its own: iron can oxidize inside the sample bottle before anyone measures it. Both are covered below.

## Dissolved vs Oxidized Iron: What Actually Differs

It is the same element in two chemical states. They behave so differently that they need different treatment.

| | Dissolved (ferrous, Fe²⁺) | Oxidized (ferric, Fe³⁺) |
|---|---|---|
| **Common names** | Clear-water iron, reduced iron | Red-water iron, particulate iron |
| **At the tap** | Clear and colorless | Orange, red or yellow tint, or visible specks |
| **In a standing glass** | Turns cloudy, then orange, then settles | Already colored; particles settle over time |
| **Physical state** | Dissolved ion | Solid particles of ferric hydroxide |
| **Where it comes from** | Low-oxygen groundwater | Ferrous iron that met air or chlorine, or rust from pipe, casing or tank |
| **What removes it** | Ion exchange within limits, or an oxidizing filter | Filtration that catches particles |
| **What it does to a softener** | Exchanges like hardness and uses up capacity | Fouls the resin bed |

[Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/iron-and-manganese-in-private-water-systems) uses the terms "reduced" and "oxidized" and gives the visual rule. Water that is clear when collected but forms orange-brown or black particles over time holds dissolved metals. Water with particles visible straight away holds metals that are already oxidized.

The two forms are not a fixed property of the aquifer. Ferrous iron becomes ferric the moment it gets enough oxygen. Where that happens, whether in the well, the pressure tank, the plumbing or your glass, decides what reaches the softener.

## The 30-Minute Glass Test, Step by Step

![Single clear glass of water standing on a white table in soft daylight, the simple setup for a timed iron glass test](https://images.pexels.com/photos/7402619/pexels-photo-7402619.jpeg)

You need two clear drinking glasses, a sheet of white paper, a phone camera and a timer.

1. **Pick the right tap.** Use the one closest to the well, ahead of any softener or filter. A hose bibb or the drain valve by the pressure tank is often the best choice. If the only taps are downstream of treatment, put the treatment in bypass first.
2. **Run the cold water** until it is cold and steady. NDSU Extension advises running water for 15 minutes before a lab sample so that it comes from the aquifer and not the pipes. A few minutes is enough for the glass test, but the longer run is worth doing if the house has been empty.
3. **Fill one glass gently**, without splashing, so you do not add air.
4. **Fill the reference glass with bottled or distilled water.** Set both side by side on the white paper in steady light. The reference glass lets you see a faint tint that you would miss when looking at one glass on its own.
5. **Photograph both glasses** from the same spot at 0, 5, 15 and 30 minutes. Keep the phone still.
6. **Leave the glass for an hour** and take a final look. Some color changes are slow.

The photographs matter more than they seem to. A slow change is easy to misremember. A set of four time-stamped pictures is also more useful than a description when you show it to an installer, or when you compare it with a lab report weeks later.

## Reading the Glass at 0, 5, 15 and 30 Minutes

![Clear hourglass with white sand on a wooden table, marking the half-hour a glass of well water needs before its iron shows its form](https://images.pexels.com/photos/9771338/pexels-photo-9771338.jpeg)

Compare your photographs against this table:

| What the photos show | What it means | Equipment path |
|---|---|---|
| Clear at every time stamp, no fixture staining | No meaningful iron in either form | Iron is not your problem. Confirm with a lab if fixtures stain anyway |
| **Clear at 0**, a faint haze by 5 to 15 minutes, orange particles settling by 30 | **Dissolved ferrous iron** | A softener is possible within its rating, and an oxidizing filter is the alternative |
| **Tinted or specked at 0**, particles settle, clearer water above them | **Oxidized ferric iron** | Filtration ahead of any softener |
| Slight tint at 0 that deepens as the glass stands | **A mix of both** | Treat it as oxidized for equipment purposes |
| Dark brown to black specks or haze | Manganese, alone or with iron | Measure manganese; it fouls resin as well |
| Yellow or tea color that does not settle, even after an hour | Tannins or organically bound iron | Neither form above. See [why softened water looks yellow](/blog/why-softened-water-looks-yellow/) |

**Things the glass test cannot tell you:**

- **It does not give you a concentration.** A faint change might be 0.5 ppm or 2 ppm. The glass tells you the form of the iron and the lab tells you the amount.
- **It does not set a timetable.** How fast the change happens varies from one well to another. The time stamps are a record for comparison, not a pass or fail.
- **Low iron is easy to miss.** A very faint tint can escape the eye, which is what the reference glass and the photographs are for.

## Where You Draw the Glass Changes the Answer

![Orange iron-stained water running over rocks where groundwater surfaces, showing dissolved iron turning to rust once it meets air](https://images.pexels.com/photos/30231276/pexels-photo-30231276.jpeg)

One glass from the kitchen tap tells you what arrives at the kitchen. It does not tell you what leaves the well. Pouring glasses from three places will usually show where the problem starts.

**At the wellhead or pressure tank compared with a downstream tap.** Suppose the wellhead glass starts clear and turns orange, but the kitchen glass is already tinted when it comes out. The iron is oxidizing inside your system, before it reaches the treatment. An older tank where air and water share the same space, a chlorinator, or an aerator installed in the wrong position can each cause this. It matters because a softener needs the water to arrive un-oxidized. Hellenbrand's softener manual says outright that air "must be kept from coming in contact with water" until it has been softened.

**First draw of the morning compared with water that has been flushed.** NDSU Extension lists rusted galvanized pipes and connections, a rusted pressure tank, and rust from the well casing or pump as sources of particulate iron. If only the first draw after the water has sat overnight comes out rusty, and a flushed sample starts clear, the iron is coming from the plumbing and not the aquifer. That is fixed in the plumbing, not with treatment equipment.

**Before and after an existing softener.** If treated water turns brown only on the morning after a regeneration, the source is iron stored in the resin bed. That is covered in [brown water after softener regeneration](/blog/brown-water-after-softener-regeneration/).

## The Sampling Trap: Iron That Oxidizes in the Bottle

![Laboratory analyzer with a rack of sample tubes beside it, where a well water sample's iron result is only as good as the bottle it arrived in](https://images.pexels.com/photos/8442374/pexels-photo-8442374.jpeg)

The chemistry that makes the glass test work also spoils lab samples. Dissolved iron does not stop oxidizing because the water is in a sample bottle.

The [EPA's guidance on groundwater sampling for metals](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-06/documents/groundwater_samp.pdf) states the problem directly. Filtering oxygen-poor groundwater "is very difficult without iron oxidation and colloid formation", which removes iron that was dissolved when the sample was drawn. A household sample carried in a half-full bottle is exposed to the same process for hours.

The trap can push the result in either direction:

- **The dissolved figure reads low, and the iron looks more oxidized than it was.** Suppose the lab filters the sample when it arrives. Iron that was dissolved at the tap but precipitated in transit is caught by the filter and counted as particulate. The gap between total and dissolved iron grows, so a well that is really ferrous looks ferric on paper. You could end up buying a filter you did not need.
- **The total figure reads low.** Iron that settles or sticks to the bottle wall is missed if only the water above it is analyzed. Equipment sized on that number will be too small.

Neither mistake shows on the report. It will print a figure to two decimal places either way.

## The Preservation Method That Prevents It

The laboratory approach is to stop the chemistry at the tap, and it is different for each measurement.

**For total iron, the bottle is acidified.** EPA Method 200.8 requires total metals samples to be left unfiltered and acidified with nitric acid to pH below 2. The requirement is quoted in an [EPA drinking water memo on acidified metals samples](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/environment/mnelap/docs/samplehold.pdf). At that pH, iron is held in solution, which is why a preserved bottle gives a far more reliable total than one left untreated. The same memo says acidification may be done at the laboratory instead of at the tap, provided the method's shipping-time instructions are followed. It also says an acidified sample must then stand for 16 hours before analysis.

**For dissolved iron, the sample is filtered in the field before air reaches it.** The EPA sampling guidance says filtration for dissolved species "should be performed in the field with no air contact and immediate preservation." Filtering at the bench a day later measures what survived the trip, not what came out of the well.

**What that means when you order a test:**

1. **Ask for a metals bottle pre-charged with preservative** for total iron. Do not rinse it before filling, because that washes out the acid. Other bottle-handling errors are covered in [water hardness sampling mistakes](/blog/water-hardness-sampling-mistakes/).
2. **Ask whether the lab can report dissolved iron from a field-filtered sample**, and how the filtering is done. If they filter at the bench, treat any "dissolved" result with caution.
3. **Fill to the top, cap it straight away, keep it cool and deliver it the same day.** Less air and less time mean less oxidation, whatever the bottle.
4. **If field filtering is not available, pair the lab's total iron with your glass photographs.** The glass shows the form and the preserved bottle gives the amount. Together they answer the equipment question.

The full test panel, including manganese, pH and hardness, is set out in [test iron and manganese before sizing a softener](/blog/test-iron-and-manganese-before-sizing/).

## Two Results That Fit Neither Box

**Iron bacteria.** NDSU Extension describes their signature as a brown slimy material, or black-brown where manganese is involved. A glass from such a well can look like an ordinary ferrous result, so the slime in tanks, toilets and fixtures is what gives it away. Neither a softener nor a standard iron filter treats the bacteria themselves.

**Organic or tannin-bound iron.** Iron attached to dissolved organic matter stays yellow or brown, does not settle and does not clear with standing. It behaves like neither form in the table and needs its own treatment approach.

If your glass does either of these things, go no further with the ferrous and ferric decision until the cause is identified.
