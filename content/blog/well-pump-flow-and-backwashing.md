Your **well pump flow rate for backwash** has to cover the softener's drain-line flow for the whole backwash and rinse, not just for a minute. That flow is set by tank diameter: about 2 gpm for a 9-inch tank, 2.5 gpm for a 10-inch and 3 to 3.5 gpm for a 12-inch. A weak well can deliver it for a few minutes from water stored in the casing and then fall short. Two bucket tests tell you which kind of well you have.

This page covers whether the well can support backwashing equipment at all. Once you know the flow it can sustain, the sizing method that starts from that number is in [sizing a softener for a low-yield well](/blog/sizing-a-softener-for-a-low-yield-well/).

## Why Backwash Is a Flow Problem, Not a Pressure Problem

Backwash reverses the water up through the resin to lift the bed, loosen it and carry trapped sediment and iron to the drain. It only works if the bed actually expands. A [WC&P design guide for softeners](https://wcponline.com/2004/03/14/learn-right-first-time-guidelines-designing-water-softeners/) says the flow "must be sufficient to expand the bed at least 50 percent". Purolite's C100E resin data sheet asks for 50 to 70 percent expansion "for at least 10 to 15 minutes".

**The valve fixes the flow, not you.** A small rubber restrictor in the drain fitting, the drain line flow control (DLFC), sets the backwash rate for the tank. Clack's WS1 service manual states that its flow controls hold their rated flow within ±10% across 20 to 125 psi. As long as the pressure stays above that floor, the valve takes the same gallons per minute whether your pressure switch is set to 30/50 or 40/60.

That is the catch on a well. The valve asks for a fixed flow for a fixed time, and the well has to supply all of it. If the supply falls short partway through, pressure drops below the flow control's working range. Backwash flow sags, the bed stops lifting and the cycle carries on as if nothing had happened.

**Nothing warns you.** No error code, no noise, no visible change. What follows is the slow sequence described in [resin channeling](/blog/water-softener-resin-channeling/): the bed packs down, water finds paths through it, and hardness starts coming through the softener earlier and less predictably.

## Pump Rate Is Not Well Yield

Most advice stops at "time a bucket at the pressure tank". That measures the pump. It does not measure the well, and on a low-yield well the difference is the whole problem.

- **Pump rate** is how fast the pump can lift and push water.
- **Well yield** is how fast the aquifer refills the well. Penn State Extension's fact sheet on [using low-yielding wells](https://extension.psu.edu/using-low-yielding-wells) defines it as "the maximum rate in gallons per minute (GPM) that a well can be pumped without lowering the water level in the borehole below the pump intake."
- **Casing storage** is the buffer between the two. The same fact sheet puts a 6-inch well at about 1.47 gallons per foot of water, so 100 feet of standing water holds about 147 gallons.
- **Pressure tank storage** is small. Penn State notes that only about 20 percent of a pressure tank's volume is usable, so a 42-gallon tank discharges about 8 gallons before the pump starts.

![Cross-section of a drilled well during a softener backwash showing static water level, the level falling as the pump outruns well yield, and the pump intake as the limit](/diagrams/well-yield-drawdown-during-softener-backwash.svg "A 30-second bucket test measures the pump. A 20-minute backwash tests the well.")

**Why a weak well can pass a quick test.** Say a pump delivers 2.5 gpm and the well yields 1 gpm. For each minute of backwash, the pump takes 1.5 gallons more than the aquifer puts back, and the difference comes out of the casing. Over a 10-minute backwash that is 15 gallons, about 10 feet of drawdown in a 6-inch well (an illustrative example, not a measured one). A well with 100 feet of water above the pump shrugs that off. A well with 15 feet does not, especially if the evening's showers and laundry have already drawn it down.

Penn State also points out that water levels move with the seasons. A well that typically has 100 feet of water "may have less than 20 feet of water storage during a drought." A backwash that works in April can fail in September.

## Test 1: Measure the Pump Rate With a Bucket

![Two blue plastic buckets beside a hose on a wall-mounted spigot, the kit for a timed bucket test of well pump flow rate](https://images.pexels.com/photos/11470450/pexels-photo-11470450.jpeg)

This gives you the ceiling. The well can never deliver more than the pump does.

1. **Use a spigot close to the pressure tank**, ideally the drain valve on the tank tee or the nearest hose bib, ahead of any filters.
2. **Run water until the pump starts.** You want to measure the pump, not water leaving the pressure tank.
3. **Open the spigot fully and time a 5-gallon bucket** from empty to full, in seconds.
4. **Convert: gpm = 300 ÷ seconds.** Do it twice and average the two.

| Seconds to fill 5 gallons | Pump rate |
|---|---|
| 30 | 10 gpm |
| 60 | 5 gpm |
| 100 | 3 gpm |
| 120 | 2.5 gpm |
| 150 | 2 gpm |

If the pump rate is already below the backwash figure for the tank you want (see the table further down), stop there. The answer is a smaller tank or a storage-buffered design. If it is comfortably above, go on to Test 2, because this number says nothing about how long the well can keep it up.

## Test 2: The Sustained Run at Backwash Rate

This is the test that answers the real question. You run the well at the softener's backwash rate for as long as a regeneration sends water to drain, and watch whether the flow holds.

1. **Find your target rate.** Use the drain line flow control figure for the tank you plan to buy (it is in the valve's specification sheet), or the table in the next section.
2. **Find the drain time.** Add the programmed backwash and rapid rinse minutes from the valve manual. If you do not have them yet, use 20 minutes. Then add 10 minutes of margin for other water use during the cycle.
3. **Set a hose bib to the target rate.** Partly close the spigot until a 5-gallon bucket fills in the matching time. At 2.5 gpm that is 120 seconds.
4. **Run it for the full time** and re-time the bucket every 5 minutes. Watch the pressure gauge at the pressure tank as you go.
5. **Run the test at the worst time.** That means the evening, after the household's heaviest use, and ideally late in a dry season. A test on a wet spring morning flatters the well.

**What passing looks like.** The bucket time stays within a few seconds of where you set it. Pressure cycles normally between cut-in and cut-out. The water stays clear.

**Stop signs.** Stop the test immediately if any of these happen:

- **Sputtering or air at the spigot.** The water level has reached the pump intake. Running on means running the pump without water.
- **Pressure falling below the cut-in setting and not recovering** with the pump running. The pump is no longer keeping up.
- **Sand, grit or cloudy water appearing** partway through, which is another sign of a well being pulled down hard.
- **Bucket times getting steadily longer**, even if pressure looks normal.

If your pump has a low-water cutoff or a pump protection control, it may shut the pump off during this test. That is a fail, not a nuisance.

The test cannot hurt the well if you stop at the first stop sign. It also cannot replace a proper yield test by a well contractor, who measures the water level itself with a meter while pumping at a set rate. If your state keeps well completion reports, the yield and static water level recorded when the well was drilled are worth looking up before you start.

## Well Pump Flow Rate vs Backwash Demand, by Tank Diameter

The backwash flow a softener needs is set by the area of the resin bed, so tank diameter drives it. The grain rating on the box does not. The WC&P design guide gives backwash rates for softener resin that rise with water temperature, because warm water expands the bed less and needs more flow to lift it:

- **4.2 gpm per square foot** at 50°F
- **5.0 gpm per square foot** at 59°F
- **5.5 gpm per square foot** at 68°F

The first three columns below multiply those rates by each tank's bed area. The last column gives published figures from manufacturer specification sheets for real residential softeners.

| Tank diameter | Bed area | At 50°F | At 59°F | At 68°F | Published figure |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 inch | 0.35 sq ft | 1.5 gpm | 1.7 gpm | 1.9 gpm | — |
| 9 inch | 0.44 sq ft | 1.9 gpm | 2.2 gpm | 2.4 gpm | 2 gpm (ECI Fleck catalog, 9×48) |
| 10 inch | 0.55 sq ft | 2.3 gpm | 2.7 gpm | 3.0 gpm | 2.5 gpm ([Pentair spec sheet](https://www.pentair.com/content/dam/extranet/web/nam/pentair/spec-sheets/english/4005646-rotary-valve-water-softener-system-spec-sheet.pdf), 10×44 and 10×54) |
| 12 inch | 0.79 sq ft | 3.3 gpm | 3.9 gpm | 4.3 gpm | 3.5 gpm (Pentair, 12×48); 3 gpm (ECI Fleck, 12×52) |
| 13 inch | 0.92 sq ft | 3.9 gpm | 4.6 gpm | 5.1 gpm | — |
| 14 inch | 1.07 sq ft | 4.5 gpm | 5.3 gpm | 5.9 gpm | 5 gpm (ECI Fleck, 14×65) |

![Bar chart of softener backwash flow needed by tank diameter at 50°F and 68°F, with an illustrative 2.5 gpm sustained well flow line marking which tanks fit](/diagrams/softener-backwash-flow-by-tank-diameter-chart.svg "Each step up in tank diameter asks the well for more flow")

**How to use the table.**

- **Well water is usually cool**, so the 50 to 59°F columns are the realistic ones for most US wells. That works slightly in a weak well's favor.
- **Your valve's flow control is what counts.** The manufacturer picks a flow control near these figures. The number stamped on it, or printed in the spec sheet, is what the valve will draw from your well.
- **Add everything that regenerates at the same hour.** An iron filter needs far more backwash than a softener, and two units backwashing together can ask for 10 gpm or more. Staggering them is covered in [pretreatment order before a softener](/blog/iron-filter-before-water-softener/).
- **Leave headroom.** A household that runs a tap or flushes a toilet during regeneration takes water from the same pump.

## Reading the Result

Compare the flow the well held steady in Test 2 with the published figure for the tank you want.

| Your result | What it means | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| Sustained flow well above the tank's backwash rate, with no stop signs | The well can support that tank | Size the softener in the normal way |
| Sustained flow only just covers it, or held only in a wet season | Marginal. It works until a dry month or a busy evening | Drop one tank diameter and accept more frequent regeneration |
| Pump rate or sustained flow below even a 9-inch tank's rate | The well cannot support a backwashing tank at its own pace | A storage-buffered design, or talk to a well contractor about yield |

## Workaround 1: A Smaller-Diameter Tank

Dropping from a 12-inch to a 10-inch tank takes the backwash demand from about 3.5 gpm to 2.5 gpm on the Pentair figures. Dropping to a 9-inch takes it to about 2 gpm.

**What you give up:**

- **Resin per inch of height.** A narrower tank holds less resin at the same height. Some of that comes back by going taller: Pentair lists 1.0 cubic foot in a 10×44 tank and 1.5 in a 10×54, both at 2.5 gpm backwash.
- **Days between regenerations.** Less resin means the softener regenerates more often. That is fine for the resin but uses a little more salt and water over a year.
- **Peak service flow.** A smaller tank passes less water at a given pressure drop. On a weak well that rarely matters, because the well itself limits peak flow.

How far you can take this, and what to do when even the smallest tank regenerates too often, is worked through step by step in [sizing a softener for a low-yield well](/blog/sizing-a-softener-for-a-low-yield-well/).

## Workaround 2: Storage-Tank Buffering

Penn State describes an intermediate storage system as a non-pressurized tank between the well and the house. The well pump fills it at a rate "slightly less than the yield of the well", and a second pump repressurizes the water for the house. A float switch controls the well pump, and a low-water cutoff in the well protects it.

With the softener downstream of the booster pump, backwash draws on stored water at the booster's rate. The well refills the tank overnight at its own pace.

**What you give up:**

- **Cost.** Penn State's 2005 fact sheet estimated a household system at under $1,500 before chlorination. Prices have risen since, so treat that as a floor rather than a quote.
- **A second pump and its electricity**, plus a second thing to fail.
- **Space and freeze protection.** The tank needs a heated space or burial below the frost line. Penn State's rule of thumb is 100 gallons of storage per person.
- **Water quality in the tank.** An open tank needs periodic cleaning, and some systems add chlorination at the storage stage.

## What This Test Cannot Tell You

- **It measures one day.** Yield and water level change with the seasons. If you can only test once, test late in the dry season.
- **It is not a certified yield test.** It tells you whether the well can supply one specific flow for one specific time. A driller's pumping test with a water-level meter tells you what the aquifer can actually yield.
- **A softener does nothing for yield.** It removes hardness. It does not add water, and no setting on the valve reduces the backwash rate below what the resin bed needs to expand.
- **Passing does not mean the water needs only a softener.** Iron, manganese, sediment and bacteria each have their own test and their own place in the treatment train.

Run both tests before you buy, not after the resin has started channeling. A well pump flow rate that holds steady through a full backwash-length run means backwashing equipment will work. One that sags means the tank diameter, or the plumbing layout, has to change first.
