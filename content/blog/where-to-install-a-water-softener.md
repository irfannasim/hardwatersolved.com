The answer to **where to install a water softener** is five requirements, and one spot has to meet all of them. It must be on the main line after the shutoff and before the water heater and every house branch. It needs a drain for regeneration water and a power outlet. The spot must never freeze or sit in direct sun, and you need room to lift the brine lid and reach the bypass.

Most homes have one or two spots that come close. This page shows you how to test them against the five requirements. You will also see how the requirements play out in four common house layouts, and which compromises you can live with. One of them, the outdoor tap branch, has to be decided before the pipes go in. For a well, the order around the pressure tank has its own page: [where to install a softener on a well water system](/blog/where-to-install-softener-on-well-system/).

## Where to Install a Water Softener: The Five Requirements

![Layout showing where to install a water softener: after the main shutoff and outdoor tap branch, before the water heater, with drain, outlet and clearance](/diagrams/water-softener-location-five-requirements-supply-line.svg "Five requirements, one spot. A location that misses any one of them costs you later.")

Manufacturer manuals list the same requirements in slightly different words. [GE Appliances' location guidance](https://products.geappliances.com/appliance/gea-support-search-content?contentId=21030) is typical. It says to install the softener "between the water inlet and the water heater" and "upstream of (before) any other plumbing connections except outside water pipes." It also asks for a nearby drain with an air gap and a spot that never drops below freezing. It warns against direct sunlight because "excessive heat from the sun may cause distortion or other damage to non-metallic parts." And the softener must not block access to the water heater or the main shutoff.

Take each requirement in turn.

### 1. Supply position: after the shutoff, before everything else

The softener treats only the water that passes through it. Any pipe that tees off the main before the softener stays hard, whatever the softener does. So the spot on the main where the softener connects matters more than which room it is in.

- **City water:** after the main shutoff and meter, and after a pressure-reducing valve if you have one.
- **Well water:** after the pressure tank and any iron or sediment stage. Never between the pump and the pressure tank.
- **Before the water heater, always.** The heater is where hardness does the most expensive damage. The one-line reason and the diagnostic for homes that have it wrong are in [should a softener go before or after the water heater](/blog/water-softener-before-or-after-water-heater/).
- **Not too close to the heater.** The [Watts PWSR softener manual](https://www.watts.com/dfsmedia/0533dbba17714b1ab581ab07a4cbb521/29606-source/639160242450000000/2915216-pdf.pdf) says to place the softener "at least 10 feet away from hot water heater to protect against hot water back-up." Hot water pushed back into a softener can damage the valve and resin. On a short run, a check valve on the heater's cold inlet is the usual fix.

### 2. Drain access

Every regeneration sends backwash and rinse water to a drain. A floor drain, a laundry standpipe, a utility sink or a sump all work. Whichever you use, leave an air gap: GE specifies 1-1/2 inches between the end of the hose and the drain. Watts adds that a drain line longer than 20 feet should be 3/4 inch. Drain lines can run uphill a limited distance, but every foot of height and length adds back pressure. A spot where the drain line is short and runs downhill is better than one that needs a long uphill run. The acceptable drain types, and the ones that are not, are covered in [where can a water softener drain](/blog/where-can-a-water-softener-drain/). Fittings, air gaps and routing are in [how to connect water softener drain tubing](/blog/how-to-connect-water-softener-drain-tubing/), and the options when no drain or outlet is in reach are in [installing a softener without a drain or outlet nearby](/blog/water-softener-with-no-drain-or-outlet/).

### 3. Power

Electronic control valves need a standard outlet within reach of the plug-in transformer. The outlet should not be switched with a light or run through a timer. A clock valve that loses power drifts, and a metered valve may stop counting. Non-electric twin-tank units skip this requirement. Nothing else does.

### 4. Temperature

This one decides the location in many homes. The Watts PWSR manual gives an operating range of 40°F to 110°F for both room and water temperature, and says damage from freezing "will void this water conditioning systems warranty." A single hard freeze can crack a valve body. A long summer in a hot, sunny spot warps plastic and ages the electronics. An interior room, a basement or a heated garage passes. An unheated garage in a cold state, or a sunny wall outside, needs protecting before it does. [Outdoor water softener installation](/blog/outdoor-water-softener-installation/) covers the freeze and heat protection that works.

### 5. Service clearance and salt access

You will lift a 40-pound bag of salt over the brine tank several times a year. You will also need to reach the bypass, the shutoffs and the drain connection when something goes wrong, and the bypass needs [exercising and testing](/blog/water-softener-bypass-valve-testing/) now and then. So leave room above the brine tank for the lid and a bag of salt, room in front of the control valve, and a clear path to carry the salt in. Measured footprints and clearances are in [how much space a water softener needs](/blog/how-much-space-does-a-water-softener-need/). Watts adds one more point that many people miss: install the softener "in a place where water damage is least likely to occur if a leak develops."

## The Outdoor Tap Decision You Only Get to Make Once

Outdoor taps are the one branch you usually want **before** the softener. Lawn watering uses large volumes of water that gain nothing from softening. Each of those gallons uses up grain capacity and, eventually, salt. Watts says to "keep outside faucets on hard water to save soft water and salt." [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/water-softening) agrees that outside spigots, toilets and basement sinks "can be bypassed," while showers, sinks and laundry should be softened. The case for each outdoor use is in [should outdoor faucets bypass a water softener](/blog/should-outdoor-faucets-bypass-a-water-softener/).

The catch is timing. Whether the outdoor taps can stay hard depends on where their pipe leaves the main. In many houses the hose bib lines come off a pipe that also feeds a bathroom or the kitchen. Splitting them after the fact means opening walls or ceilings. So decide at installation:

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Outdoor taps already tee off before the chosen spot | Install as planned. They stay hard |
| Outdoor taps share a branch with indoor fixtures | Either re-pipe the hose bibs to the hard side now, or accept softened water outside and size for it |
| Irrigation system on the house supply | Tee it off before the softener. Softening irrigation water only adds sodium to your soil |
| You want soft water at one outdoor tap, for washing a car | Run a separate line from the soft side to that tap |

If the outdoor taps end up on softened water, add their use to your daily gallons when you size the softener. A summer of watering can add more to the load than the whole house uses indoors.

Two related branches are worth deciding at the same time. **A kitchen drinking water tap** on unsoftened water needs its own line from the hard side. That line is far easier to run while the main is open; whether you want one is covered in [should a kitchen drinking tap bypass the softener](/blog/should-a-kitchen-tap-bypass-the-softener/). **A fire sprinkler feed**, if your house has one, follows the sprinkler designer's and local code requirements, not the softener installer's. Confirm where it connects before choosing the tie-in point.

## Checking a Spot Before You Buy

Before you order a softener, stand in the spot you have chosen and confirm each requirement with a tape measure and a flashlight:

1. Trace the main from the entry to this spot. Is there any tee between them? If so, where does that branch go?
2. Find the drain. How far away is it, and is its inlet higher than the valve?
3. Find the outlet. Is it switched, or on a circuit that trips often?
4. Check the lowest temperature this spot reaches in winter, and whether sun reaches it in summer.
5. Measure the height above the brine tank position and the path for carrying salt in.

If all five answers are good, the location is settled. If one fails, the rest of this page shows which failures you can work around.

## Applied to Four Real House Layouts

The five requirements work out differently depending on where the main enters and where the heater sits. These are the four layouts that cover most US homes:

| Layout | Where the main usually enters | Best softener spot | Acceptable compromise | Compromise that causes trouble |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Full basement** | Through the foundation wall, often near the front | Beside the main entry, near the floor drain or laundry | Moving it a few feet along the main to reach a drain | Moving it past the tee that feeds the upstairs, or into an unheated corner |
| **Slab with attached garage** (common in the South and West) | Rises in the garage wall, often beside the water heater | In the garage, between the entry and the heater | An unheated garage in a mild climate, with the valve kept out of sun | An unheated garage where winter lows fall below freezing, with no protection |
| **Crawl space with utility closet** | Enters the crawl space, then rises into a closet or laundry room | In the closet or laundry room where the main comes up | Re-routing the main a short way to reach a laundry standpipe | Putting the softener in the crawl space itself, where nobody will carry salt |
| **Well house or pump room** | From the pressure tank in an outbuilding or basement | Indoors, after the pressure tank and filters | An insulated, heated well house with a drain | An unheated well house where the pump alone keeps the room above freezing |

Two patterns hold across all four. The best spot is nearly always **where the main enters, or where it rises**, because that is the only point certain to be ahead of every branch. And the most common installation fault is moving the softener away from that point to reach an outlet or a drain. The softener then ends up downstream of a tee, and part of the house stays hard. For the full breakdown by room, see [softener in a basement, garage, crawl space or closet](/blog/softener-in-basement-garage-crawl-space-or-closet/).

## Compromises You Can Live With, and Ones You Cannot

Almost no house meets all five requirements perfectly. Some compromises cost you a little. Others cost you a repair.

**Compromises that are fine:**

- **A drain line that runs uphill a moderate distance**, within the height and length the valve manual allows.
- **An extension of the transformer's low-voltage cord** to reach an outlet. Most manuals allow this with the right wire gauge.
- **A long pipe run from the entry to the softener**, as long as nothing tees off it except hard-water branches you chose.
- **A mild-climate garage.** It is fine as long as the valve is shaded and a rare cold snap can be handled with an insulated cover and heat.
- **A tight closet**, if the brine tank can slide out for filling.

**Compromises that cause problems later:**

- **Any spot downstream of a house branch.** The branch stays hard for good. This and the other faults installers repeat are listed in [common water softener installation mistakes](/blog/common-water-softener-installation-mistakes/).
- **Any spot that can freeze without protection.** One cold night cracks the valve or tank, and freeze damage is usually outside the warranty.
- **Direct sun on the control valve or brine tank.** Plastic warps and the electronics age.
- **A brine tank you cannot reach with a bag of salt.** It will run empty, and hard water will come back unnoticed.
- **A drain line that is too long or too high** for the valve's drain flow. Backwash is then weak, and the resin bed slowly fouls.
- **A short direct pipe from the heater outlet side**, with no check valve, where hot water can back up.

## What the Right Location Cannot Fix

Location only lets the softener do its job. A well-placed unit that is undersized will still run out before regeneration. A softener in the right spot still does not remove bacteria, lead, nitrate or sulfide, and does not correct acidic water. Iron beyond what the resin can handle needs its own filter upstream (see [should a water filter go before or after a softener](/blog/filter-before-or-after-water-softener/)). Before choosing a spot, test the water. Size the unit from the results, and check that the chosen location has room for any pretreatment stage the test calls for. That stage usually sits right beside the softener.
