## How Twin-Tank Water Softeners Switch Tanks

Every twin does the same job: one resin tank softens, the other waits, and a valve swaps them when the working tank is spent. What differs is what tells the valve to swap and what powers the swap. There are three designs on the residential market.

| Switching design | Example | What triggers the swap | Power | Where to buy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electronic metered, one valve head | Fleck 9100SXT | Turbine meter counts gallons; the digital controller compares them to the capacity you program | Plug-in transformer | Online, including Amazon |
| Mechanical metered, one valve head | Fleck 9100 (mechanical) | Meter turns a gear-driven program wheel set to the capacity | Plug-in, drives the motor | Online stores; we found no Amazon listing |
| Water-powered, non-electric | Kinetico twin-tank models | Mechanical meter driven by the flowing water | None | Dealers only |

Pentair's [Fleck 9100 twin valve technical sheet](https://pentair.eu/sites/default/files/collaterals/technical_sheet_9100_en.pdf) lists both a mechanical and an electronic version of the same valve body. It describes the operating principle in two lines: "continuous soft water provision with only one tank in use, other being in regeneration or stand-by" and "regeneration with softened water". Both versions share the valve body and the twin-tank principle. The difference is the controller: the SXT is a digital, programmable head with a wider range of settings, while the mechanical version has no electronics beyond its motor. Pentair lists a 24 V electrical rating for the series, so both need power.

Kinetico takes the motor out altogether. Its [resource page on non-electric softeners](https://resourcecenter.kinetico.com/solution-center/non-electric-water-softener-vs-electric-water-softener/) explains that a mechanical water meter measures use and starts regeneration on demand, with the moving water supplying the power. That suits a house with no outlet near the main line, or one on a generator. The trade is that you buy through a dealer, at a dealer price, with no Amazon option.

### What flows where during regeneration

![Twin-tank water softener flow during regeneration: hard water enters one valve, Tank A softens for the house, Tank B regenerates with soft water and brine, rinse water to drain](/diagrams/twin-tank-water-softener-flow-during-regeneration.svg "The regenerating tank is rinsed with soft water from the tank still in service")

On a single-tank softener, the valve passes untreated water to the house for the whole regeneration, which the guide on [using water during regeneration](/blog/can-you-use-water-during-regeneration/) times stage by stage. On a twin, the house keeps drawing from the tank in service. The regenerating tank draws its brine and its rinse water from that same softened supply, so its bed is rinsed with water that is already soft.

On the Fleck 9100 in its standard downflow setup, Pentair's sheet lists the cycle order as brine and slow rinse, backwash, rapid rinse, then brine tank refill. Brining comes before backwash, the reverse of the backwash-first order common on single-tank valves.

The fair comparison is per tank. A twin only ever softens with one tank at a time, so a 48k twin replaces a 48k single, not a 96k single. Using AFWFilters store prices for the same Fleck brand and the same 10% crosslink resin on both sides:

|---|---|---|---|
| 32k (1.0 cu ft) | about $759 | about $1,324 | about $565 (74%) |

| 64k (2.0 cu ft) | about $889 | about $1,825 | about $935 (105%) |

*Store prices checked 1 October 2026. Both columns include a brine tank and bypass.*

You get part of the premium back in running cost. A single tank holds back a day's load as reserve and recharges it whether or not it was used. A twin runs each tank to exhaustion. The [single tank vs twin tank comparison](/blog/single-tank-vs-twin-tank-water-softeners/) sets out that efficiency case in full. On moderate water the saving is a few dollars of salt a year and never repays the premium. Above about 30 gpg it grows, but the main reason to buy a twin is still the uninterrupted soft water.

Space is the other cost. Both tanks hang off one valve head on a fixed connector, so they sit side by side at a set spacing:

| Size | Single tank footprint | Twin footprint |
|---|---|---|
| 32k | One resin tank + brine tank | Two resin tanks side by side + brine tank |
| 48k | One 10 in tank + 14 in square brine tank | Two 10 in tanks + 14 in square brine tank |
| 64k | One 12 in tank + brine tank | Two 12 in tanks + brine tank |

Allow roughly one extra tank diameter of wall width, plus space at the front to reach the valve and pour salt.

## Sizing a Twin: Per Tank, Not Combined

![Decision chart for twin-tank water softeners: buy a single tank if hard water during regeneration is acceptable, non-electric twin if no power, else size the 9100SXT per tank by daily grains](/diagrams/twin-tank-water-softener-buying-decision-and-size.svg "Size each tank to last about five to seven days at the top of your load")

Twin listings sometimes advertise the two tanks added together, such as "96,000 grains" for a 48k-per-tank system. Ignore that figure. Size one tank from your daily hardness load using the method in [how to size a water softener](/blog/how-to-size-a-water-softener/), then buy the twin with that tank size.

Two differences from single-tank sizing work in the twin's favour:

1. **No reserve.** The standby tank is the reserve, so the full usable capacity of each tank counts.
2. **Short intervals are less of a problem.** A single tank that regenerates every two days spends a lot of time sending hard water to the house. A twin that swaps every two days does not. You still pay for the salt and rinse water, so aim for five to seven days per tank.

Use real usable capacity, not the box rating. At 9 lb of salt per cubic foot, standard resin gives about 26,000 grains per cubic foot, not the 32,000 behind the label (see [advertised vs usable softener capacity](/blog/advertised-vs-usable-softener-capacity/)).

## Before You Install a Twin

- **Power.** Both Fleck versions need an outlet within reach of the transformer cord.
- **Drain.** One drain line serves both tanks. It must handle the backwash rate of one tank: 2.2 to 4 gpm across these picks.
- **Bypass.** Fit a bypass valve so you can isolate the system for service without shutting off the house.
- **Ceiling height.** The valve sits on top of both tanks. A 10 x 54 inch tank with the head fitted needs roughly 62 inches of headroom, plus room to lift the head off.
- **Programming.** Enter the per-tank capacity at the salt dose you choose, not the combined figure. If you enter the doubled number, each tank is asked to deliver twice its capacity and hard water breaks through before the swap.

## Who Should Buy None of These

- **Households that sleep at night and use little water then.** A single tank regenerating at 2 a.m. gives the same soft water for roughly half the price.
- **Homes with no outlet at the install point** that cannot run one. A non-electric dealer system is the realistic option.
- **Wells with iron above a few ppm or any oxidised iron.** These picks use standard high-capacity resin. Deal with the iron first. AFWFilters also lists fine-mesh "Iron Pro" twin builds.
- **Tight utility closets.** If two resin tanks and a brine tank will not fit with service access, a cabinet or single tank is the honest answer.
- **Renters.** A three-tank system with a drain connection is not something to fit without the owner's permission.

A twin-tank softener is a convenience upgrade, not a softening upgrade. Buy one when nonstop soft water is worth about $600 to $900 to you, and size it one tank at a time.
